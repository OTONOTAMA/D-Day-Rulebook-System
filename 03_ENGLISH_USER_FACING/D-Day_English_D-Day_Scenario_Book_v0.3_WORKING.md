# D-Day Scenario Book

**Campaign-History / Reader-Value Expansion v0.3 — Working Draft**

This book is the Scenario Book for the Normandy campaign beginning on 6 June 1944.

It is not a second Player Rulebook.

The Player Rulebook tells you how to resolve procedures. The Rules Companion explains the deeper logic of those procedures. This Scenario Book answers a different set of questions:

- What historical campaign are we trying to represent?
- What problems did the opposing commanders actually face?
- Which of those problems does the Scenario preserve?
- What should each player notice when turning history into decisions on the map?

Historical names, formations, beaches, towns, and operations in this book are used to explain the campaign. They do **not** automatically become game units, setup locations, factors, reinforcement values, or victory values. Those belong to the Scenario's authoritative data and map implementation.

Where the current Scenario design has not yet fixed such data, this book says so rather than inventing an answer.

# Part One — The Historical Normandy Campaign

# 1. Before D-Day — Why Invade Western Europe?

## 1.1 The Strategic Situation in 1944

By the spring of 1944, Nazi Germany was fighting a continental war on multiple fronts.

The Soviet Union was applying enormous pressure in the east. In Italy, Allied forces had already established a major land campaign. From Britain, the Western Allies possessed a growing concentration of American, British, Canadian, and other Allied forces, shipping, aircraft, equipment, and logistical infrastructure.

Yet the central strategic problem remained unresolved:

> How could the Western Allies put a large army into north-western Europe, keep it supplied, and turn the landing into a campaign that Germany could not contain?

Crossing the Channel was only the first step.

A successful invasion required a sequence:

1. gain lodgments on the coast;
2. survive the first German reactions;
3. connect separate landing areas;
4. build supply capacity;
5. bring in additional formations;
6. expand the lodgment into operational depth;
7. break the German ability to contain the bridgehead;
8. convert the breakout into pursuit.

That sequence is important for this Scenario because it explains why the campaign cannot be reduced to “win the beach battle.”

The historical Normandy campaign was a sustained struggle over **time, space, reserves, roads, ports, supply, and the ability to turn local success into operational freedom**.

## 1.2 Why Normandy?

The Allies needed a coast that could be reached from southern Britain, supported by air power, supplied across the Channel, and expanded into a large continental lodgment.

The Pas-de-Calais offered the shortest Channel crossing and appeared to be an obvious invasion area. That very obviousness made it heavily watched and strongly defended.

Normandy offered a different balance.

Its coastline was within Allied air reach. It offered beaches suitable for major landings. The Cotentin Peninsula contained the port of Cherbourg, an important potential logistical asset. Inland, the region connected toward the Seine basin, Paris, and the broader road network of France.

Normandy was not an easy choice. It was a choice whose difficulties the Allies believed they could solve.

That distinction matters.

A campaign plan is rarely built by finding a place with no problems. It is built by choosing a set of problems that can be managed better than the alternatives.

## 1.3 Sort Out the Names First — Overlord, Neptune, Bodyguard, and Fortitude

Several famous names are often used together, and a reader can easily lose track of what each one means.

### Operation Overlord

**What it was:** the Allied operation to invade north-western Europe through Normandy and establish the continental campaign.

**Why it matters:** Overlord was larger than the beach assault. It included the landing and the campaign required to establish and expand the lodgment.

For this book, “Overlord” is useful when we are talking about the invasion as an operational campaign rather than only the hours of 6 June.

### Operation Neptune

**What it was:** the assault phase of Overlord, especially the movement across the Channel, naval support, airborne opening, and landings that placed the invasion force onto the Normandy coast.

**Why it matters:** Neptune helps separate the immediate invasion mechanics from the larger campaign.

The Scenario follows the same conceptual distinction. Amphibious and airborne operations matter greatly, but they are the beginning of the land campaign, not a complete campaign by themselves.

### Operation Bodyguard

**What it was:** the broader Allied deception effort designed to confuse German assessments about the place and timing of Allied offensives.

**Why it matters:** an invasion does not begin only when troops move. It also begins when the defender forms expectations about where those troops will move.

### Operation Fortitude

Fortitude was a major part of the deception architecture.

It is commonly divided into:

- **Fortitude North**, which encouraged German attention toward a possible threat to Norway;
- **Fortitude South**, which helped sustain the impression that the decisive cross-Channel invasion might come in the Pas-de-Calais.

The important campaign effect was not that every German commander believed every deception story.

The important effect was uncertainty.

German forces and command attention could not be concentrated solely on Normandy with perfect confidence.

### What the player should notice

The Scenario does not try to reproduce the entire intelligence and deception campaign as a separate mini-game.

Instead, the historical lesson is preserved more broadly:

- the defender must deploy before knowing the attacker's final operational commitment;
- reserves matter;
- where the defender places strength is part of the campaign problem;
- information and timing affect the value of those reserves.

## 1.4 The Campaign Had Already Begun — The Transportation Plan

Before 6 June, Allied air power attacked rail centers, bridges, transportation nodes, and parts of the French transport network.

This program is often discussed under the **Transportation Plan**.

### When

The major preparatory effort developed in the months before D-Day, especially during the spring of 1944.

### What it tried to do

The purpose was not simply to destroy trains.

The operational purpose was to make it harder for German formations to move rapidly toward the eventual invasion area.

If rail movement is disrupted, bridges are damaged, and movement corridors become less reliable, the defender may still possess reserves but have greater difficulty putting them in the right place at the right time.

### Why it mattered

This is a recurring theme in Normandy:

> a formation can exist on the order of battle and still fail to matter at the decisive place because it arrives too late.

That idea is central to the Scenario's treatment of operational mobility and air interdiction.

Air power is not only a way to modify one combat.

It can attack **time**.

## 1.5 Operational Meaning

The pre-invasion story establishes four campaign ideas that will return throughout this book.

First, the invasion is not a single battle. It is a chain of operational problems.

Second, the defender faces uncertainty before the attacker commits.

Third, transportation and reserve movement can matter as much as printed combat strength.

Fourth, success on the coast only matters if it can be converted into a sustainable campaign inland.

The Scenario is built to preserve those problems without turning the player into an intelligence staff, railway scheduler, or naval logistics administrator.

# 2. The German Defensive Problem — Defend the Coast or Preserve the Reserve?

## 2.1 The Atlantic Wall Was Not One Uniform Wall

The phrase “Atlantic Wall” can create the image of a continuous, equally strong fortified line running along the coast.

That is misleading.

The coastal defenses varied greatly.

Some sectors contained strongpoints, obstacles, artillery positions, prepared defenses, and troops positioned close to likely landing areas. Other areas were weaker, less complete, or held by formations of uneven quality.

The operational problem was therefore not:

> Is the wall strong or weak?

It was:

> Where is it strong, where is it thin, and can the defender reinforce the place that becomes decisive?

This is one reason the Scenario should not treat every coastal hex as identical.

At the same time, exact strongpoints, factors, fortification values, and setup hexes remain Scenario and map data. This book does not invent them.

## 2.2 The Fundamental German Dilemma

German commanders did not agree completely on how the invasion should be defeated.

One broad approach emphasized defeating the invasion close to the coast before Allied naval fire, air power, and logistics could build an overwhelming lodgment.

Another emphasized keeping powerful mobile reserves far enough back that they could be concentrated against the true main landing after it became clear.

Both ideas had logic.

The coastal approach reduced the time available for an attacker to build strength.

The reserve approach preserved freedom to respond to uncertainty.

But both also had risks.

A reserve held too far away might arrive too late.

A reserve committed too close to the wrong coast might be pinned or bypassed.

A strong coastal deployment might spread valuable formations across too many possible landing sectors.

The defense therefore faced a problem with no perfect answer.

## 2.3 Uncertainty About the Main Invasion

Even after Allied troops began landing in Normandy, German command still had to interpret what those landings meant.

Was Normandy the main invasion?

Was it a preliminary attack before a larger landing in the Pas-de-Calais?

Would another major assault follow elsewhere?

The deception campaign increased the cost of making the wrong judgment.

This mattered because every formation committed to Normandy was a formation that might not be available against another feared landing.

Delay in commitment was therefore not simply “incompetence.”

It was partly the result of a defender trying to make decisions under incomplete information.

## 2.4 Command and Reserves — Having a Unit Is Not the Same as Being Able to Use It

A reserve is valuable only if it can be committed effectively.

Several things can reduce that value:

- unclear authority;
- delayed release;
- uncertain information;
- damaged transportation;
- Allied air attack;
- distance;
- congested roads;
- the need to defend more than one sector.

This is why the Scenario treats reserves and operational movement as more than a count of combat factors.

A powerful formation in the wrong place is not equivalent to the same formation arriving one Player Turn earlier.

## 2.5 Operational Meaning

For the German player, the opening problem is not simply “put the strongest units on the coast.”

The questions are more difficult:

- How much strength must be close enough to resist the first landings?
- How much must remain mobile?
- Which routes must remain open for the reserve?
- Which areas can be defended by delay rather than by maximum strength?
- How much risk can be accepted before the Allied plan is known?

That is why the Scenario uses a German-first setup followed by visible Allied invasion planning.

The sequence preserves the historical problem of commitment under uncertainty without making setup a simultaneous hidden-information exercise.

# 3. D-Day — 6 June 1944

## 3.1 The Invasion Began from the Air

The campaign did not begin only when landing craft reached the beaches.

Airborne forces were dropped on the flanks of the invasion area during the night and early morning.

Their missions included securing routes, disrupting German movement, protecting the edges of the amphibious lodgment, seizing or denying bridges and causeways, and creating confusion in the defender's rear.

Airborne operations were risky.

Units could become scattered.

A force that landed deep behind enemy positions could influence important routes but could also become isolated from the main invasion.

This creates the same operational tradeoff represented in the Scenario:

> position can be gained before connection is secure.

## 3.2 The Five Landing Beaches

The Allied amphibious assault came ashore across five principal beach sectors.

From west to east:

- **Utah**
- **Omaha**
- **Gold**
- **Juno**
- **Sword**

American forces landed on Utah and Omaha.

British forces landed on Gold and Sword.

Canadian forces landed on Juno.

The names are famous, but the operational point is more important than memorizing them.

The five beaches were not one continuous front at the moment of landing.

They were separate assault areas that had to survive, expand, and eventually connect.

### Utah

Utah was the westernmost landing sector, on the Cotentin side of the invasion area.

The landing became closely connected to the airborne battle inland, because exits from the flooded coastal zone and routes toward the peninsula mattered immediately.

### Omaha

Omaha became the most difficult of the five main beach assaults.

Defenses, terrain, disrupted landing organization, and heavy losses created a dangerous situation in the opening hours.

Yet the beachhead survived and expanded.

The important operational lesson is that severe tactical difficulty does not automatically mean operational failure if the lodgment remains viable.

### Gold, Juno, and Sword

The British and Canadian beaches on the eastern side of the invasion faced their own defensive positions and inland objectives.

Their later operational problem was strongly connected to Caen, the road network around it, and the arrival of German armored reserves.

## 3.3 “Success” Did Not Mean “Everything Went to Plan”

D-Day succeeded in the most important sense:

the Allies established major lodgments in Normandy and were not thrown back into the sea.

But that does not mean the timetable unfolded exactly as intended.

Some objectives were not reached.

Some formations landed in unexpected places.

Some areas advanced farther than expected.

Other areas moved much less.

This distinction is useful in play.

A Scenario opening should not be judged by whether it reproduces a historical script hex by hex.

The important question is:

> Has the invading player created a viable operational position from which the campaign can continue?

## 3.4 German Reaction on D-Day — Understanding the Invasion While It Was Happening

The German response had to develop while commanders were still determining the scale and meaning of the landings.

Local defenders fought immediately.

Reserves began moving.

But the larger command problem remained:

- Which landing sector was most dangerous?
- Was Normandy the decisive Allied effort?
- Which reserves should be released?
- How much strength should be committed before the possibility of another landing had been dismissed?

The famous German armored counterattack near the eastern landing area showed that German forces could react aggressively.

But reaction was not the same as restoring the original coastal position.

By the end of 6 June, the basic strategic fact had changed:

the Allies had an army ashore.

The campaign was now about whether that army could expand faster than Germany could contain it.

# 4. From Separate Landings to One Lodgment

## 4.1 The Real Problem Began on 7 June

Once the beaches survived, the invasion immediately became a land campaign.

The Allies needed to:

- widen the separate beachheads;
- secure inland exits;
- connect neighboring lodgments;
- protect supply routes;
- bring additional formations ashore;
- prevent German counterattacks from splitting the landing area.

The Germans needed to do the opposite:

- keep beachheads narrow;
- preserve gaps between Allied sectors;
- threaten road connections;
- feed reserves into the battle;
- prevent the invader from gaining operational depth.

This is why the days after D-Day matter as much as the assault itself.

## 4.2 Carentan and the Western Lodgment

In the western part of the invasion area, the connection between Utah and Omaha mattered greatly.

Carentan sat in a region of roads and low-lying ground between the two.

The fighting around Carentan in mid-June helped connect what had begun as separated American lodgments.

### Why it mattered

A connected lodgment is more than a larger colored area on the map.

Connection can improve:

- lateral movement;
- mutual support;
- reserve flexibility;
- supply routing;
- the ability to shift effort between sectors.

The Scenario therefore encourages the player to think about connecting areas, not simply capturing isolated coastal hexes.

## 4.3 The Eastern Problem

On the eastern side, the Allied landing areas were more closely connected to the struggle for Caen and the nearby road network.

The original timetable had envisioned faster progress.

Instead, German armored formations and prepared defensive positions helped produce a prolonged battle.

The Allied problem became:

> How do we expand the eastern bridgehead and keep German mobile strength tied down while still creating room for a wider breakout?

## 4.4 Operation Perch and Villers-Bocage — Trying to Turn the Western Flank of Caen

### When

Operation Perch developed during the first half of June 1944.

The best-known episode, the fighting around **Villers-Bocage**, occurred on 13 June.

### Where

West of Caen, in the sector where Allied forces hoped to exploit gaps and pressure the German defense from the flank.

### Objective

The operation aimed to help capture or outflank Caen by moving around the western side rather than relying only on a direct push through the eastern defenses.

### What happened

British forces advanced through an opening toward Villers-Bocage.

The movement produced one of the campaign's most famous armored actions.

German armored resistance, including heavy tanks, disrupted the thrust.

The attempt did not produce the hoped-for operational turning movement around Caen.

### Why it mattered

Villers-Bocage is often remembered as a tank battle.

At campaign scale, its greater importance is different.

It shows how quickly an apparent gap can close once reserves arrive.

The opening existed.

The Allies moved to exploit it.

German mobile strength reacted.

The opportunity disappeared before it could become a decisive operational rupture.

### How the Scenario abstracts the problem

The game does not need to reproduce the exact tank encounter.

It needs to preserve:

- the value of gaps;
- the speed with which a reserve can change a sector;
- the importance of roads;
- the difficulty of converting a local opening into sustained operational depth.

## 4.5 The Real Race of June — Allied Build-Up versus German Reinforcement

During June, both sides were racing.

The Allies were landing additional troops, vehicles, fuel, ammunition, engineers, and supplies.

The Germans were moving reinforcements toward Normandy while fighting under Allied air pressure and transport disruption.

The contest was not simply:

> Who has more forces?

It was:

> Who can make usable combat power arrive where it matters, at the right time, while keeping the force supplied?

That is one of the central questions of the Scenario.

# 5. The Cotentin and Cherbourg — Capturing a Port Is Not the Same as Using It

## 5.1 Why Cherbourg Mattered

The Allied invasion required enormous quantities of supply.

Open beaches could support the early campaign, but major ports offered the possibility of much greater and more durable capacity.

Cherbourg, at the northern end of the Cotentin Peninsula, was therefore an important objective.

Capturing it would not win the campaign by itself.

But without sufficient logistics, every later success would become harder to sustain.

## 5.2 Cutting the Peninsula and Driving North

American forces expanded westward and northward from the Utah sector.

A key operational objective was to cut the Cotentin Peninsula so that German forces in the north could not be reinforced easily from the south.

Once the peninsula was cut, the attack could turn toward Cherbourg.

### Why this sequence mattered

The direct distance to a target is not the only measure of an operation.

Cutting the peninsula first changed the conditions around the target.

It reduced the defender's freedom and made the later battle for Cherbourg more favorable.

This is a useful Scenario lesson:

> sometimes the best route to an objective is to change the operational geometry around it first.

## 5.3 A Captured Port Can Still Be a Broken Port

Cherbourg was captured in late June.

But German demolition had badly damaged the port facilities.

The Allies had gained the location.

They had not instantly gained full logistical value.

Repair, clearance, organization, and transport had to follow.

This distinction is deliberately important in the rules architecture:

- control of a port;
- active / usable port state;
- supply or entry capacity;

are separate concepts.

The historical campaign gives a clear reason for keeping them separate.

## 5.4 Mulberries and Supply Across the Beaches

The Allies did not rely on Cherbourg alone.

Artificial harbor systems known as **Mulberries**, direct beach unloading, landing craft, and a large logistics organization supported the invasion.

This matters because a campaign can survive even before a major permanent port is functioning — but at a cost and with limits.

The Scenario therefore treats beaches, Beachheads, ports, and later logistical infrastructure as related but not identical forms of access.

## 5.5 The Great Storm of 19–22 June — Nature Attacks the Logistics Plan

A severe Channel storm struck in mid-June.

It damaged landing craft, disrupted unloading, and badly affected the artificial harbor system, particularly the American Mulberry at Omaha.

### Why it mattered

This episode is a reminder that logistics plans are not abstract promises.

They depend on physical infrastructure and conditions.

The invasion force could not assume that every planned supply method would remain available at full effectiveness.

### How the Scenario uses the lesson

The common rules do not create a universal random “June storm” table.

Weather or special logistical disruption belongs to Scenario design if needed.

The broader operational lesson is already preserved by the importance of:

- supply sources;
- port state;
- Beachheads;
- capacity;
- route vulnerability.

# 6. The Battle for Caen — Failure to Advance Can Still Matter

## 6.1 Caen as an Early Objective

Caen was a major road center on the eastern side of the invasion area.

Its capture had been expected early.

Instead, fighting around Caen continued for weeks.

This became one of the defining features of the Normandy campaign.

A simple narrative might say:

> The British failed because Caen was not captured on schedule.

That is too narrow.

The real operational story involves terrain, German armored reserves, Allied build-up, repeated offensives, and the way fighting in one sector could influence opportunities elsewhere.

## 6.2 Why the Front Moved So Slowly

Several factors contributed:

- German armored formations were committed heavily in the Caen sector;
- defensive positions south and west of the city were difficult to break;
- villages, ridgelines, river lines, and open ground shaped attack routes;
- Allied attacks often required large preparation;
- German reserves could be drawn into one battle even when the territorial result was limited.

The result was an attritional struggle in which modest map movement could still have major operational meaning.

The key question becomes:

> What did an offensive force the enemy to commit, even if the attacker did not gain the full planned objective?

## 6.3 Operation Epsom — Turning West of Caen and Drawing in German Armor

### When

26–30 June 1944.

### Where

West of Caen, across the Odon sector.

### Principal attacking force

British Second Army, with Eighth Corps at the center of the offensive.

### Objective

The attack aimed to cross the Odon, push toward high ground south-west of Caen, and threaten the city and German positions from the flank.

It was not simply a frontal assault on Caen.

### What happened

British forces gained ground and established positions across the Odon.

German armored formations were committed heavily to contain the offensive.

The operation did not achieve a decisive breakthrough to the south.

### Result

Territorial gains were limited compared with the most ambitious objectives.

Yet the German response consumed important armored reserves and helped keep major German mobile strength tied to the eastern battle.

### Why it mattered

Epsom is a strong example of why operational offensives cannot be judged only by the final front line.

An operation can fail to reach its deepest objective and still change:

- where the enemy reserve is;
- what the enemy must defend;
- how quickly the enemy can respond elsewhere.

### Scenario abstraction

The game does not contain an “Epsom rule.”

It preserves the underlying problem through combat sequence, reserve movement, roads, supply, and the value of forcing enemy concentration.

## 6.4 Operation Windsor — Fighting for Carpiquet

### When

4–5 July 1944.

### Where

Immediately west of Caen, around the village and airfield at Carpiquet.

### Principal attacking force

Canadian forces.

### Objective

Secure Carpiquet and its airfield approaches as part of the effort to improve the Allied position west of Caen.

### What happened

The fighting was hard and localized.

Canadian troops captured the village, while control of the broader airfield position remained contested until subsequent operations.

### Why it mattered

Windsor is smaller than Goodwood or Charnwood, but it shows how local terrain and approach positions could matter in the larger battle for Caen.

The operation helped prepare the conditions for the next phase.

### Player lesson

Not every useful battle captures a famous city or breaks a front.

Some battles are valuable because they improve the position from which the next operation will be launched.

## 6.5 Operation Charnwood — Forcing the Fight into Caen

### When

8–9 July 1944.

### Where

Northern Caen.

### Principal attacking forces

British and Canadian formations.

### Objective

Break into and secure the northern part of Caen, pushing German defenders back from the city's northern approaches.

### What happened

The attack was preceded by major bombardment and followed by a broad ground assault.

Allied forces entered Caen and secured the city north of the Orne.

German forces withdrew to positions south of the river.

### Result

Caen was no longer the same German-held barrier it had been in June, but the battle for the ground south and east of the city continued.

### Why it mattered

Charnwood changed the geography of the eastern battle.

It did not instantly open the road to a full operational breakout.

The Allies had taken important urban ground while the Germans still held defensive depth to the south.

### Scenario lesson

Capturing an important location changes the campaign.

It does not automatically eliminate the enemy's ability to defend beyond it.

## 6.6 Operation Goodwood — The Great Armored Offensive East of Caen

### When

18–20 July 1944.

### Where

East and south-east of Caen.

### Principal attacking force

British Second Army, using a major concentration of armored formations.

### Objective

Drive south from the Orne bridgehead, break through German defenses, gain operational space, and place heavy pressure on German armored forces.

The exact intended depth of exploitation has long been debated, but operationally the attack was clearly a major attempt to transform the eastern battle.

### What happened

The offensive began after enormous air bombardment.

British armored formations advanced across open ground but encountered mines, anti-tank defenses, villages, ridgelines, and German armored resistance.

Substantial ground was gained, but no decisive breakout followed.

### Result

Goodwood did not produce the dramatic armored breakthrough that its scale might suggest.

It did, however, force Germany to keep and commit powerful formations in the eastern sector.

### Why it mattered

Goodwood is another warning against evaluating an operation using only one metric.

If the only question is:

> Did British armor break into open country?

the answer is no.

If the question is:

> Did the attack gain ground, inflict pressure, and hold German mobile strength in the east?

the answer is more complicated.

### Scenario lesson

A large offensive may have value even when it does not generate maximum territorial advance.

The opponent's reserve posture is part of the result.

## 6.7 Operation Atlantic — Canadian Pressure South of Caen

### When

18–21 July 1944, overlapping with Goodwood.

### Where

South of Caen, toward the approaches to Verrières Ridge.

### Principal attacking force

Canadian formations.

### Objective

Advance south of Caen, secure important ground, and support the wider Allied effort to push the German defensive line away from the city.

### What happened

Canadian forces crossed into the southern Caen area and fought toward difficult defensive positions near Verrières Ridge.

German resistance remained strong.

### Result

The operation gained important ground but did not solve the Verrières problem.

### Why it mattered

Atlantic shows that Goodwood was not an isolated British tank attack.

The eastern front contained simultaneous and connected pressure from British and Canadian forces.

When reading the campaign, avoid treating named operations as separate boxes.

They overlap in time and influence the same reserve system.

## 6.8 Operation Spring — A Costly Attempt to Break the Verrières Position

### When

25 July 1944.

### Where

South of Caen, around Verrières Ridge and nearby villages.

### Principal attacking force

Canadian forces.

### Objective

Break through the German defensive position south of Caen and continue the pressure created by earlier July operations.

### What happened

The attack met strong resistance.

Canadian forces suffered heavy losses and did not achieve the intended breakthrough.

### Why it mattered

Spring shows the danger of attacking a strong position when the defender can concentrate strength and the operational conditions are unfavorable.

At the same time, the eastern pressure remained part of the wider Allied campaign at the exact moment the American breakout effort began farther west.

## 6.9 “Caen Was Not Taken on Time” Is Not Enough

The Caen battle consumed time and lives.

It frustrated Allied plans.

But it also fixed major German armored strength in the eastern sector.

That matters when we reach Operation Cobra.

The campaign should not be read as two unrelated stories:

- British and Canadian forces “stuck” near Caen;
- American forces later “won” in the west.

The pressure in the east and the eventual opportunity in the west were connected by the German reserve problem.

That is the operational view this Scenario wants the player to understand.


# 7. Bocage — Short Distances That Took a Long Time to Cross

## 7.1 The Hedgerow Country

Much of the American sector in western Normandy was bocage country.

The word refers to a landscape of small fields enclosed by thick hedgerows, sunken lanes, banks, and limited lines of sight.

On a strategic map, the distances are small.

At ground level, the terrain breaks those distances into repeated local obstacles.

A field edge can become a defensive line.

A road can channel movement.

A short advance may require many separate fights.

## 7.2 Why the American Advance Slowed

The bocage reduced the value of simply having more vehicles or more theoretical mobility.

Formations had to move through a landscape that favored concealment, ambush, and local defense.

The terrain also made coordination harder.

An attacker might win one position and still face another hedge line immediately beyond it.

The result was attritional progress.

The campaign map did not move quickly even though very large forces were engaged.

### Scenario lesson

A hex scale of roughly 10 kilometers cannot reproduce every hedgerow.

It should not try.

The operational effect belongs in the map's terrain structure, movement costs, combat conditions, and the broader difficulty of turning local success into rapid operational movement.

## 7.3 Toward Saint-Lô

Saint-Lô became an important focus in the western battle.

The fighting around it formed part of the difficult American advance through the bocage.

By mid-July, the cumulative struggle had created a different strategic situation.

The Allies had more forces ashore.

The Germans had suffered sustained pressure.

The question was becoming:

> Can the attacker stop fighting the bocage battle on the defender's terms and create a true breakout?

That question leads directly to Operation Cobra.

# 8. Saint-Lô and Operation Cobra — From Attrition to Breakout

## 8.1 Operation Cobra — Breaking Out of the Bocage

### When

Operation Cobra began on 25 July 1944.

### Where

The American sector west of Saint-Lô.

### Principal attacking force

U.S. First Army.

### Objective

Create a concentrated rupture in the German front and break out of the constricted bocage battle into terrain where operational movement could expand much more rapidly.

### What happened

A massive air bombardment preceded the ground attack.

The opening was dangerous and imperfect — including tragic friendly casualties from bombing — but the German front in the target sector was eventually ruptured.

American forces then began to exploit south and west.

### Result

The character of the western campaign changed.

Instead of fighting for one hedgerow line after another, American forces gained increasing operational freedom.

### Why it mattered

Cobra was not simply another successful attack.

It changed the kind of war being fought in the west.

Before Cobra:

- short movement;
- close terrain;
- repeated attrition;
- narrow tactical opportunities.

After the rupture:

- wider maneuver;
- deeper advances;
- more vulnerable flanks;
- greater importance of roads and reserves;
- faster pressure on the German command system.

### Scenario abstraction

This is one of the clearest examples of why the Scenario must support changing operational tempo.

The same unit and the same Movement Allowance can behave very differently when the front opens.

## 8.2 The Moment the Game Changes

A breakout creates new opportunities and new problems.

The attacker suddenly has more legal and useful routes.

The defender has more frontage to cover.

Supply routes lengthen.

Reserves may no longer be able to respond everywhere.

The map can stop behaving like a continuous line and begin behaving like a network of roads, strongpoints, blocking positions, and pursuit routes.

A good operational Scenario should allow that change to emerge from play rather than trigger it through a scripted “breakout event.”

## 8.3 After the Breakthrough — Follow the Plan or Exploit the Opportunity?

Once movement opened, the Allied command faced choices.

Should forces continue toward the original planned objectives?

Should they push into Brittany?

Should they turn east?

Should they try to destroy German forces before those forces could escape?

These are not questions with one obvious answer.

The faster the advance, the harder the logistics problem can become.

The farther the exploitation, the more exposed the flanks.

The campaign becomes less about breaking one line and more about allocating mobility.

## 8.4 Operation Bluecoat — British Pressure on the Eastern Side of the Breakout

### When

Operation Bluecoat began on 30 July 1944 and continued into early August.

### Where

The British sector south of Caumont, east of the American breakout zone.

### Principal attacking force

British Second Army.

### Objective

Pressure the German front, seize important terrain, and limit the German ability to shift forces freely against the American breakout.

### What happened

British forces advanced through difficult terrain and engaged German armored formations.

The fighting around places such as Mont Pinçon and Vire helped maintain pressure on the German center.

### Result

Bluecoat did not become a Cobra-style breakout.

Its operational value lay partly in forcing the Germans to fight on another front while the American rupture expanded.

### Why it mattered

The Normandy battle was now strongly interconnected.

The American breakout cannot be understood only by looking west.

German reserves still had to respond to pressure from British and Canadian sectors.

### Player lesson

One offensive can matter because it prevents the enemy from solving a crisis somewhere else.

That is why the Scenario values reserve location, Operational Movement, and the ability to create simultaneous problems.

---

# 9. Avranches — When the Operational Space Suddenly Opens

## 9.1 From Breakthrough to Corridor

The American advance reached the Avranches area at the base of the Cotentin Peninsula.

This created access from Normandy into a much wider operational space.

The geography now changed dramatically.

The Allies were no longer confined to a shallow bridgehead against a relatively continuous German front.

They could move into Brittany, southward, or eastward.

The German defense now had to respond to multiple possible axes.

## 9.2 The Front Stops Looking Like a Line

In a contained bridgehead, both sides can often think in terms of a continuous front.

After Avranches, that becomes less useful.

The important objects become:

- road junctions;
- corridors;
- reserve positions;
- open flanks;
- supply routes;
- exits from pockets;
- locations that connect one operational area to another.

This is the kind of transition that a hex-and-counter operational system is particularly good at representing.

The board can show the moment when “holding the line” stops being the only meaningful defensive concept.

## 9.3 Brittany or East? — Success Creates More Choices

A breakthrough does not remove decision-making.

It creates more of it.

Brittany contained ports that had long been part of Allied logistical planning.

But German forces farther east were becoming increasingly vulnerable.

The attacker therefore had to compare different kinds of value:

- secure long-term logistics;
- pursue and destroy the enemy;
- seize terrain before the defender can reorganize;
- maintain supply to a rapidly moving force.

The Scenario should allow the Allied player to face a similar kind of choice without forcing a historical answer.

History provides the problem.

The player supplies the solution.

---

# 10. Mortain — Being Able to Counterattack Is Not the Same as Should Counterattack

## 10.1 Operation Lüttich — The German Counterattack Toward Avranches

### When

The counteroffensive began on 7 August 1944.

### Where

Around Mortain, aimed westward toward the Avranches corridor.

### German name

**Operation Lüttich.**

It is often referred to simply as the Mortain counterattack.

### Objective

Cut the American corridor around Avranches and separate the forces that had broken out into Brittany and the wider French interior from the main Allied position in Normandy.

In operational terms, the target was not just a town.

It was the narrow connection that made the breakout usable.

### What happened

German armored forces attacked westward.

They achieved local progress but were unable to cut the Avranches corridor.

American resistance, Allied air power, the broader operational situation, and the limited freedom available to German forces all worked against the attack.

### Result

The counteroffensive failed.

German mobile strength was committed at a moment when the overall front was becoming increasingly dangerous.

## 10.2 Orders versus Operational Reality

The counterattack illustrates a recurring command problem.

A counterstroke may look attractive because:

- the enemy has advanced quickly;
- a narrow corridor appears vulnerable;
- armored formations still exist;
- a successful strike could transform the situation.

But a counterattack also commits reserves.

If it fails, those reserves may be badly placed for the next crisis.

## 10.3 Why It Was a Dangerous Gamble

By early August, the Allies possessed growing freedom of movement and strong air support.

The German front was already under pressure elsewhere.

A major westward counterattack therefore risked sending valuable formations into an operation that did not solve the larger encirclement danger developing to the east.

The issue is not that counterattack is inherently wrong.

The issue is timing.

### Player lesson

A reserve is most valuable before commitment because it still has options.

Once committed, it becomes strong in one place and unavailable everywhere else.

## 10.4 Operational Meaning

Mortain gives the German player one of the most important lessons in the campaign:

> Do not judge a counterattack only by the local combat odds.

Also ask:

- What does success actually accomplish?
- What happens if the attack stalls?
- Which reserve is no longer available afterward?
- What route or escape corridor becomes less protected?

The same principle applies to the Allied player.

Every major commitment has an opportunity cost.

---

# 11. Falaise — When the Escape Route Becomes the Objective

## 11.1 The Opportunity for Encirclement

After the American breakout and the failure of the Mortain counterattack, German forces in Normandy faced the danger of being trapped.

American forces were moving around from the south and west.

British and Canadian forces continued pressure from the north.

The campaign problem changed again.

For the Allies, the central question was no longer only:

> How do we advance?

It became:

> Can we close the exits before the German armies escape?

For the Germans:

> How much of the force can still be extracted before the corridor closes?

This is a different kind of operational contest.

A road or town can matter because it is an exit, not because it is intrinsically valuable.

## 11.2 Operation Totalize — Night Armored Columns Toward Falaise

### When

Operation Totalize began on the night of 7–8 August 1944.

### Where

South of Caen, on the axis toward Falaise.

### Principal attacking force

First Canadian Army, with Canadian and other Allied formations.

### Objective

Break through the German defenses south of Caen and drive toward Falaise.

### What happened

The attack used innovative night movement and armored columns, supported by heavy firepower.

Initial gains were made, but the operation did not immediately reach Falaise.

German resistance and the difficulty of sustaining the advance slowed the attack.

### Why it mattered

Totalize was part of the northern arm of the developing encirclement.

The closer Canadian and Allied forces moved toward Falaise, the narrower the German escape problem became.

### Scenario lesson

A successful offensive may matter because it reduces the defender's future options rather than because it immediately destroys a large number of units.

## 11.3 Operation Tractable — Falaise and the Exit Toward Trun

### When

Operation Tractable began on 14 August 1944.

### Where

South toward Falaise and beyond, as the encirclement tightened.

### Principal attacking force

First Canadian Army.

### Objective

Renew the drive, take Falaise, and continue toward the routes by which German forces were escaping eastward.

### What happened

Allied forces advanced through difficult fighting.

Falaise was taken.

The operational focus increasingly shifted beyond the town itself, toward Trun and the remaining escape corridor.

### Why it mattered

This is an important distinction.

Falaise was significant.

But once the larger encirclement developed, **the exits mattered even more than the city**.

The campaign was becoming a contest over routes.

## 11.4 The Closing Corridor — Trun, Chambois, and Coudehard

As Allied forces converged, the remaining German escape corridor narrowed around the area of Trun, Chambois, and Coudehard.

Polish, Canadian, American, and other Allied forces contributed to closing or constricting the routes.

German formations fought desperately to escape eastward.

The result was not a perfectly sealed pocket from one instant to the next.

The corridor narrowed, opened under pressure, narrowed again, and was contested violently.

Many German troops escaped.

Large amounts of equipment were lost.

Other formations were destroyed or captured.

## 11.5 “The Pocket Closed” Was Not a Single Instant

Maps often show a clean encirclement arrow and a closed ring.

Operational reality is messier.

A pocket contains:

- units already trapped;
- units still moving toward the exit;
- units protecting the exit;
- roads clogged by traffic;
- opposing forces trying to close the gap;
- local counterattacks trying to reopen it.

That is why this Scenario should not treat encirclement as a decorative map label.

The important rules are the ordinary ones:

- movement;
- control;
- Zones of Control;
- retreat;
- supply;
- roads;
- reserve position.

## 11.6 Operational Meaning

Falaise demonstrates a mature operational principle:

> sometimes the most important target is not the enemy unit, but the route the enemy needs.

A player who only looks for the strongest enemy stack may miss the decisive road.

---

# 12. The Seine, Paris, and the End of the Normandy Campaign

## 12.1 German Withdrawal

After the collapse of the Normandy position and the Falaise battles, German forces withdrew eastward.

The campaign increasingly became one of pursuit.

Allied forces moved toward the Seine.

The operational challenge was now very different from the bocage battles of June and July.

The front was more open.

Distances were greater.

The defender was trying to escape and reconstitute.

The attacker was trying to move fast enough to prevent a stable new defense.

## 12.2 Victory Creates New Problems for the Winner

Rapid pursuit sounds easier than fighting a prepared defensive line.

It creates its own difficulties.

The farther the Allied armies advanced:

- the longer the supply routes became;
- the more transport was required;
- the greater the strain on maintenance;
- the more important road prioritization became;
- the farther the armies moved from the beaches and original depots.

The campaign therefore ends with a paradox:

> operational success can create logistical crisis.

## 12.3 Pursuit and Supply — The Red Ball Express

### When

The Red Ball Express began in late August 1944, after the Normandy breakout had created a fast-moving pursuit.

### What it was

A large truck-based supply system designed to move fuel, ammunition, food, and other supplies forward from depots toward rapidly advancing Allied armies.

### Why it was necessary

Rail infrastructure was damaged.

Ports and depots were not always close to the advancing front.

The armies were moving faster than the original logistics network could comfortably support.

### Why it mattered

The Red Ball Express is not part of the initial D-Day assault.

It matters to this book because it shows the final stage of the same logistical story that began on the beaches.

At first the question was:

> Can we get enough supply ashore?

By late August it had become:

> Can we move enough supply hundreds of kilometers forward fast enough to sustain pursuit?

### Scenario lesson

A logistics system should not only punish a failed attack.

It should also constrain success when the front outruns its support structure.

## 12.4 What Did the Normandy Campaign Achieve?

By late August 1944:

- the Allied lodgment in France had survived;
- the Normandy defensive position had been broken;
- major German forces had suffered severe losses;
- the Allies had crossed into a much broader operational space;
- Paris was liberated on 25 August;
- the campaign was transitioning into the pursuit across France.

This Scenario begins with the problem of getting ashore.

The history ends with a completely different problem:

how to exploit a victory faster than the logistical system can comfortably support.

That transformation — from invasion, to lodgment, to attrition, to breakout, to encirclement, to pursuit — is the campaign arc the game should be capable of producing.

# Part Two — What the Game Preserves from the Campaign

# 13. What Should the Game Preserve from Normandy?

The Scenario cannot reproduce every historical event.

Nor should it.

The useful question is:

> Which campaign problems created decisions at the level represented by the game?

The most important are:

- committing the German defense before the final Allied invasion plan is known;
- creating and sustaining the initial lodgment;
- connecting separate invasion areas;
- moving reserves under time pressure;
- using roads as movement, supply, and reinforcement infrastructure;
- balancing coastal defense against mobile reserve;
- expanding Beachheads;
- bringing ports into useful service;
- managing operational air power;
- preventing or creating isolation;
- converting combat results into positional advantage;
- deciding when to preserve or commit reserves;
- deciding whether an advance is sustainable;
- determining victory through operational control, not simply casualties.

Those are the campaign problems the rules are intended to make visible.

# 14. What Is Compressed and What Remains a Player Problem?

## 14.1 Tactical Fighting on D-Day

The real beach battles included enormous tactical detail:

- individual strongpoints;
- obstacle belts;
- naval gunfire;
- engineer actions;
- company-level movement;
- local command breakdown;
- weapon-by-weapon effects.

The Scenario does not reproduce all of that.

It compresses the landing into an operational procedure connected to the normal Combat System.

What remains for the player is the higher-level problem:

- where to allocate lift;
- where to accept opposed assault;
- how wide a lodgment to create;
- how to connect the landing to the inland campaign.

## 14.2 Command Structure

The historical command system contained many headquarters, personalities, national chains of command, and political constraints.

The Scenario does not require the player to reproduce every historical command friction rule.

What it preserves is the operational effect where it matters:

- reserve timing;
- commitment;
- player authority;
- sequence;
- Scenario-defined restrictions if needed.

## 14.3 Physical Detail of Logistics

Real logistics involved:

- ships;
- landing craft;
- dumps;
- truck companies;
- pipelines;
- railways;
- port repair;
- engineering;
- fuel allocation;
- maintenance.

The game compresses those details into:

- Supply Sources;
- Reach;
- Capacity;
- Entry Sources;
- Beachheads;
- port state;
- roads;
- Scenario-defined resource values.

The point is not to simulate every truck.

The point is to force the player to care whether the army can continue operating.

## 14.4 The Air War

The historical air campaign was immense.

The Scenario does not place every squadron on the map.

It instead asks the player to allocate operational air resources between uses such as:

- immediate combat support;
- Operational Interdiction;
- prepared breakthrough support.

The historical effect is preserved as a decision rather than as an air-operations subgame.

# 15. History Is Not a Script

The purpose of historical grounding is not to force the player to reproduce the historical sequence.

The Allied player is not required to conduct a historical Operation Cobra.

The German player is not required to launch a historical Mortain counterattack.

The Canadian player role does not exist separately from the Allied player role merely because Canadian forces were historically important.

Historical operation names in Part I explain what happened.

They are not mandatory Scenario commands.

The Scenario should instead create a situation in which similar operational questions can arise:

- Is there a sector where concentration can rupture the front?
- Can a reserve close the gap?
- Is an exposed corridor worth counterattacking?
- Should a strongpoint be attacked or bypassed?
- Is a port worth the time required to take and repair?
- Can an airborne position survive until link-up?
- Should the defender trade ground for time?

If play produces a different campaign because players answer those questions differently, that is not a failure of historical design.

It is the point of a historical wargame.

# Part Three — Playing This D-Day Scenario

# 16. Scenario Framework

The Scenario represents the Normandy campaign beginning on 6 June 1944.

The current canonical framework is:

- exactly two opposing player roles;
- Allied player role;
- German player role;
- German pre-game setup first;
- Allied observation and invasion planning second;
- Game Turn 1 then begins;
- Allied Player Turn first;
- German Player Turn second;
- one Game Turn represents approximately three calendar days;
- approximately 10 kilometers per hex;
- primary operational formations are generally regiment / brigade scale;
- divisions function mainly as higher groupings;
- battalions appear independently only when operationally meaningful.

There is no fixed universal end date in the current Scenario design.

The game ends through the Scenario's victory / game-end architecture, concession, or mutually agreed draw.

Exact map, formation roster, factors, setup hexes, reinforcement schedule, and other concrete data remain separate Scenario-development items.

# 17. Allied Player Brief

## 17.1 Your Strengths

The Allied side begins with several important advantages:

- the ability to choose the invasion plan after observing the German setup;
- powerful amphibious and airborne opening options;
- major logistical potential if Beachheads and ports are developed;
- strong air resources;
- the possibility of building increasing operational strength as the lodgment expands.

Your greatest long-term strength is not one combat factor.

It is the ability to turn a successful landing into a growing system of:

**Beachheads → roads → supply → reinforcement → operational depth.**

## 17.2 Your Weaknesses

At the beginning, your strength is constrained by access.

A formation that has not yet landed cannot help the ground battle.

A formation ashore but trapped near the coast may have little operational freedom.

An airborne formation can be valuable but fragile.

A port can be captured but not yet usable.

Your first problem is therefore not “destroy the German army.”

It is:

> build a continental position that can keep getting stronger.

## 17.3 Six Questions Before You Commit

1. Which coastal sectors give the best combination of landing viability and inland access?
2. Where can airborne forces change the ground problem rather than merely create an isolated pocket?
3. Which landing areas must connect quickly?
4. Which roads matter for supply and reinforcement after the landing?
5. Where are the German mobile reserves?
6. Which German Victory Objective Hexes must you be able to threaten or seize early enough to avoid an automatic German advantage under the Scenario's victory architecture?

Exact objective values remain Scenario data.

The planning principle is already clear:

your opening must create a **legal opportunity** to prevent an immediate German victory.

## 17.4 Common Allied Mistakes

### Winning the beach and stopping there

A landing is only the first stage.

### Dropping airborne forces too deep

A dramatic position that cannot survive or link up may have less value than a safer position with a clear operational purpose.

### Ignoring supply because the first turn looks strong

The invasion must still function at later Supply Checkpoints.

### Treating every German unit as a mandatory target

Some defenders matter because of where they stand, not because they must be destroyed.

### Taking an Objective that cannot be held until the official check

Occupation and durable control are different problems.

---

# 18. German Player Brief

## 18.1 Your Strengths

You act first in setup.

You can shape the initial defense before the Allied invasion plan is committed.

You may be able to use:

- coastal defense;
- depth;
- road blocks;
- reserve positions;
- local counterattack;
- delay.

Your strongest tool is often not raw combat power.

It is forcing the Allied player to spend time solving several problems at once.

## 18.2 Your Weaknesses

You must commit before knowing the final Allied plan.

A formation placed too far forward may be bypassed or fixed.

A formation placed too far back may arrive too late.

Roads can be threatened by airborne forces and air interdiction.

Once the Allied lodgment grows, your freedom can shrink quickly.

## 18.3 Six Questions Before Setup

1. Which coastlines require immediate strength?
2. Which inland roads must remain open for reserves?
3. Where can a small force delay more than its printed combat value suggests?
4. Which sectors can trade space for time?
5. Which reserve locations preserve more than one response option?
6. Which German Victory Objectives must remain defensible at the formal check times?

## 18.4 Common German Mistakes

### Putting everything on the coast

This can create strength at the first contact but destroy reserve flexibility.

### Keeping everything too far back

A reserve that arrives one Player Turn late may never recover the lost space.

### Counterattacking because a counterattack is possible

Ask what the counterattack accomplishes if it succeeds and what reserve is lost if it fails.

### Holding a position after its operational value is gone

A strong defensive hex can become a trap if supply or retreat routes disappear.

---

# 19. German Setup First, Allied Invasion Planning Second

## 19.1 Formal Opening Sequence

The intended opening structure is:

1. German pre-game deployment.
2. German setup becomes visible.
3. Allied player examines the visible setup.
4. Allied player commits the Scenario-defined pre-game invasion plan.
5. Pre-game procedures end.
6. Game Turn 1 begins.
7. Allied Player Turn.
8. German Player Turn.

This is not simultaneous secret setup.

It is not an extra German Player Turn.

It is Game Setup.

## 19.2 Why Use This Sequence?

The sequence creates a controlled asymmetry.

The German player must decide where to place strength before seeing the final Allied commitment.

The Allied player then receives information, but not unlimited freedom.

The Allied player still has to work within:

- Scenario-defined landing opportunities;
- lift;
- airborne limits;
- map geography;
- supply;
- victory requirements.

The result is a meaningful opening decision for both sides.

## 19.3 What the German Player Should Think About During Setup

- coast versus depth;
- roads;
- reserve travel time;
- objective defense;
- ability to respond to more than one landing sector;
- risk of airborne disruption;
- which forces are too valuable to be fixed immediately.

## 19.4 What the Allied Player Should Think About While Observing

Do not simply search for the weakest beach.

Look for a **campaign path**.

Ask:

- If I land here, where can I go next?
- Can neighboring lodgments connect?
- Is there an inland road?
- Where can the German reserve respond?
- Can I maintain supply?
- What Objective pressure can I create by the first official checks?

The weakest coast can still lead to the worst campaign position.

# 20. Game Turn 1 Walkthrough

This walkthrough uses the Scenario structure but deliberately avoids inventing unresolved setup or unit data.

## 20.1 Game Turn 1 Begins

All Game Setup and pre-game planning must be complete.

The Allied Player Turn begins.

## 20.2 Initial Invasion

The Initial Invasion occurs during the first Allied Special Operations Step before the first Allied Supply Checkpoint.

This timing matters.

Airborne and Amphibious Operations can create the first on-map Allied position before supply is formally checked.

## 20.3 Airborne Operations

For each airborne operation:

- confirm the formation is eligible;
- confirm lift and reference-distance requirements;
- choose a legal drop location;
- resolve placement;
- record its special supply treatment for the drop turn.

The Scenario may permit designated initial reference hexes because ordinary friendly ground reference units may not yet exist.

## 20.4 Amphibious Landings

Before resolving the first assault:

- allocate lift;
- assign participating formations;
- assign all assault hexes;
- lock the assignments.

Then resolve each landing hex completely.

Some may be unopposed.

Some may require opposed Amphibious Assault.

Beachhead creation follows the relevant common rules and Scenario values.

## 20.5 The First Supply Checkpoint

Now check the on-map Allied formations under the supply rules.

This is an important transition.

The opening Special Operations created the position.

The Supply Checkpoint determines whether that position is connected to a functioning operational support system.

## 20.6 Ground Movement

Eligible formations now move.

The Allied player should think about:

- linking landing areas;
- opening roads;
- reducing dangerous gaps;
- protecting Beachheads;
- approaching Objectives;
- avoiding an advance that cannot be supported.

## 20.7 Combat

Resolve attacks one at a time.

The first combat can change:

- defender position;
- retreat routes;
- control;
- later attack options.

Do not plan the Combat Step as though the board will remain unchanged after every die roll.

## 20.8 End of the Allied Player Turn

Before ending, ask:

- Which landing areas are connected?
- Which remain isolated?
- Which routes must survive the German response?
- Which German Objectives are now threatened?
- Where can the German reserve counterattack?

## 20.9 The First German Player Turn

The German player now responds to the actual invasion.

This is the first moment when the pre-game defensive plan is tested against the revealed Allied plan.

Priorities may include:

- contain a Beachhead;
- cut a connection;
- destroy an isolated airborne formation;
- preserve a reserve;
- protect an Objective;
- counterattack a dangerous corridor.

## 20.10 End of the German Player Turn

Apply any victory check that the Scenario assigns to this timing.

This is why the Allied player cannot evaluate success only at the end of the Allied Player Turn.

A position must survive the German reply when the victory architecture requires it.

# 21. Airborne Operations — Changing the Coastal Battle from Inland

## 21.1 The Real Advantage of Airborne Forces

Airborne forces can place operational pressure where ordinary ground forces cannot yet reach.

They can:

- seize an important location;
- interfere with reserve movement;
- threaten roads;
- protect a flank;
- help create a link between separate parts of the invasion.

Their advantage is time.

They arrive early.

Their weakness is connection.

## 21.2 What Makes a Good Airborne Position?

A good position should answer a campaign question.

Examples:

- Does it protect an inland exit from the coast?
- Does it threaten a reserve route?
- Does it hold a crossing?
- Does it help connect to the amphibious force?
- Can the unit survive until link-up?

A location that is merely “deep” is not automatically useful.

## 21.3 The Danger of Dropping Too Deep

The farther an airborne force is from friendly ground forces:

- the harder link-up becomes;
- the more dangerous isolation becomes;
- the more enemy effort may be needed to destroy it — which can be good;
- but the easier it may be for the operation to become disconnected from the main campaign.

The right depth depends on purpose.

## 21.4 Historical Connection

The historical airborne operations on D-Day show exactly this tension.

Some missions sought bridges and routes.

Others protected the flanks of the amphibious landings.

Scattered drops sometimes reduced immediate concentration but also created confusion for the defender.

The Scenario does not attempt to simulate every drop pattern.

It preserves the operational choice between **early position and later connection**.

---

# 22. Amphibious Operations and Beachheads — Turning Invasion into Land Campaign

## 22.1 Amphibious Lift Is Force Allocation

Lift capacity determines how much combat power can be converted from offshore potential into on-map force.

Choosing one powerful formation may strengthen one assault.

Choosing several smaller formations may create a broader lodgment.

The important question is:

> What shape of land campaign do I want after the first landing?

## 22.2 Opposed Landing

An opposed landing uses the common Combat System with specific Amphibious adjustments.

The goal is not to create an entirely separate beach tactical game.

The goal is to resolve the assault and immediately connect the result to:

- occupation;
- supply;
- Beachhead creation;
- later reinforcement;
- inland movement.

## 22.3 Surviving Defenders

A defender that survives an opposed Amphibious Assault is protected from being attacked again in the later normal Combat Step of that same Player Turn.

This preserves time consistency.

The beach defense should not be fought twice inside one operational time segment.

## 22.4 Beachheads

A Beachhead can function as a temporary operational base according to Scenario-defined values.

Its importance can include:

- supply;
- reinforcement entry;
- continued inland operations.

A Beachhead should therefore be judged by what it supports after the landing, not merely by the fact that a landing occurred.

A narrow lodgment with no useful inland exit may remain fragile even if many formations are ashore.

---

# 23. The Logistics Campaign — Turning Beaches, Ports, and Roads into Combat Power

## 23.1 Supply Is Not Rear-Area Administration

Supply determines whether success can continue.

The Allied player can win a beach battle and still create a bad campaign if the resulting position cannot be sustained.

The German player can fail to destroy a formation directly but still gain value by cutting the route that supports it.

Supply creates important terrain outside the immediate front.

## 23.2 The Value of Ports

A port may provide:

- supply;
- Entry Capacity;
- long-term logistical depth.

But only if its state and Scenario conditions allow those functions.

Historical Cherbourg is the obvious warning.

Capturing the location did not instantly restore full port capability.

## 23.3 The Meaning of Beach Supply

Beach unloading and Beachheads allow the invasion to operate before major ports become fully useful.

That is essential to the historical campaign.

But a Beachhead is not limitless.

Capacity and persistence matter.

## 23.4 Roads Have Three Jobs

A road can support:

1. movement;
2. supply;
3. reinforcement / reserve flow.

That is why a road junction can matter even when it has no Victory Objective marker.

A player should learn to see roads as infrastructure connecting several systems at once.

---

# 24. Air Superiority — Taking Time from the Enemy

## 24.1 “Where Should Air Power Be Used?”

The Scenario treats air power as an operational resource.

The player decides where its effect matters most.

## 24.2 Help the Current Battle

Combat Air Support changes the current Combat Band.

This is the most visible use.

It can make an important assault more reliable.

## 24.3 Make the Enemy's Next Position Worse

Operational Interdiction can prevent an enemy formation from using Operational Movement.

This can be more important than one Combat Band shift.

A reserve delayed by one Player Turn may fail to:

- save an Objective;
- seal a gap;
- cut a Beachhead;
- escape a pocket.

## 24.4 Heavy Breakthrough Bombardment

Heavy Breakthrough Bombardment represents a prepared, exceptional concentration of support.

It is separate from ordinary Tactical Air Points.

Its purpose is to give the Scenario a way to represent rare breakthrough preparation without making every combat an air-allocation exercise of the same type.

## 24.5 The Player's Question

Do not ask only:

> Where can I gain the biggest combat modifier?

Also ask:

> Where can I change the enemy's timing?

The history of Normandy repeatedly shows that timing can be as decisive as damage.

---

# 25. Deciding the Campaign — What Does It Mean to Win?

## 25.1 The Two Sides Have Different Objective Groups

The current victory architecture uses separate Objective Hex groups for the Allied and German sides.

Exact Objective Hexes, group sizes, and thresholds remain Scenario data.

This book does not invent them.

## 25.2 Check Timing Matters

The Allied decisive condition is checked at the end of the German Player Turn.

The German decisive condition is checked at the end of the Allied Player Turn.

This asymmetry matters.

It means each side may have to **hold** a required condition through the opponent's opportunity to act.

## 25.3 Destroying the Enemy Is Not the Same as Winning

Casualties matter.

They make later operations easier or harder.

But unless a Victory Condition explicitly uses them, losses are not the final score.

A side can win many combats and still fail to control the required operational space.

## 25.4 No Fixed Universal End Date

The current Scenario design does not impose a fixed final calendar date or maximum turn count as the universal game-end mechanism.

The game continues until:

- a Scenario-defined decisive victory condition ends it;
- a player concedes;
- both players agree to a draw.

This makes positional and operational resolution more important than racing toward an arbitrary turn number.

# Part Four — Player Guide and Operational Examples

# 26. Allied Player Guide — Think in Three Campaign Stages

## 26.1 Stage One: Survive

Your opening task is to create viable lodgments.

Ask:

- Can the landing survive the first German response?
- Are the airborne positions useful and supportable?
- Are the Beachheads functioning?
- Are the inland exits open?

## 26.2 Stage Two: Connect

Separate successes are fragile.

Connect them.

Link roads.

Create mutual support.

Protect supply.

Reduce gaps that the German player can exploit.

## 26.3 Stage Three: Expand

Once the lodgment is stable, create operational depth.

Now the questions become:

- Where is the German reserve?
- Which road or sector can be turned into a breakout?
- Which Objectives can be held through the official check?
- How far can the offensive advance without outrunning supply?

## 26.4 Allied Self-Diagnosis

At the end of each Allied Player Turn, ask:

- Did I create more operational freedom than last turn?
- Is my supply structure stronger or weaker?
- Is the German reserve being forced to react?
- Am I taking positions I can hold, or only positions I can reach?
- Is the campaign becoming one connected system, or several isolated local successes?

---

# 27. German Player Guide — What to Hold and When to Give Ground

## 27.1 Stage One: Keep the Invasion Divided

The early German opportunity is to prevent the Allied landing areas from becoming one stable system.

A gap can be valuable even if no major Allied unit is destroyed.

## 27.2 Stage Two: Buy Time

Every delayed road connection can matter.

Every forced Allied attack can matter.

Every Player Turn in which a reserve remains available can matter.

The German side does not need to hold every hex.

It needs to make Allied expansion expensive in time and operational flexibility.

## 27.3 Stage Three: Preserve the Reserve

As the lodgment grows, the reserve becomes even more valuable.

Do not commit every mobile formation simply because a battle is available.

Keep enough freedom to answer the next crisis.

## 27.4 Counterattack Decision

Before counterattacking, ask:

1. What operational problem does success solve?
2. What happens if the attack succeeds only partially?
3. What happens if the attack fails?
4. Which reserve is no longer available elsewhere?
5. Does the attack improve the victory position, or only produce local damage?

Mortain is the historical warning.

# 28. Extended Conceptual Example — From Invasion Plan to First German Response

This example uses generic formations and locations only.

## 28.1 German Setup

The German player places:

- some formations near likely landing areas;
- a mobile reserve near a central road network;
- lighter forces protecting secondary approaches.

The German player does not know the final Allied invasion plan.

## 28.2 Allied Observation

The Allied player sees:

- one coastal sector with strong immediate defense;
- another with lighter defense but poor inland roads;
- a central reserve able to respond to more than one area.

The Allied player therefore avoids asking only:

> Where is the weakest beach?

Instead the player asks:

> Which landing can become a sustainable campaign position?

## 28.3 Airborne Operation

An airborne formation is placed near an inland road junction.

The location is not the deepest legal drop.

It is chosen because it can interfere with German reserve movement and still offers a realistic path to ground link-up.

## 28.4 Amphibious Landings

Two coastal sectors are selected.

One receives the strongest assault concentration.

The other receives enough strength to create a second lodgment and force German attention.

All assignments are fixed before the first assault is resolved.

## 28.5 Supply Checkpoint

One lodgment is secure and supplied.

The second is legal but narrow.

The airborne force is temporarily supplied under the special opening rule but may become vulnerable if no ground connection is created later.

## 28.6 Movement

The Allied player uses movement primarily to:

- connect the stronger Beachhead to an inland road;
- reduce the gap toward the second landing;
- move toward the airborne position.

The farthest possible move is not always selected.

## 28.7 Combat

The first attack targets a modest German formation blocking the road between the two lodgments.

The German unit is not the strongest unit on the map.

It is attacked because its position matters.

The combat forces a retreat.

A Post-Combat Advance opens the road.

A later planned attack becomes unnecessary.

## 28.8 German Decision

The German reserve can counterattack the road connection.

But doing so would leave a Victory Objective sector weak.

The German player must choose between:

- restoring separation between Allied lodgments;
- preserving the Objective;
- retaining reserve flexibility.

## 28.9 Result

No side has “won the campaign” in Game Turn 1.

But the central operational structure is already visible:

- landing;
- connection;
- supply;
- roads;
- reserve response;
- victory timing.

That is the type of opening the Scenario is designed to produce.

# 29. Operational Case Studies

## 29.1 Weak Coast, Bad Inland Position

A landing sector has light defense.

Behind it are poor roads and difficult access.

A second sector has stronger coastal defense but much better inland connections.

The easy beach may create the harder campaign.

## 29.2 Strong Coast, Good Connection

A difficult landing can still be worthwhile if success gives:

- immediate road access;
- connection to another lodgment;
- a strong route toward Objectives.

Evaluate the land campaign after the landing.

## 29.3 Isolated Airborne Force

An airborne formation has successfully landed near an important road.

It is now separated from friendly ground forces.

The player must decide whether to:

- prioritize link-up;
- use the airborne position as a temporary blocking force;
- accept the risk and continue the main operation elsewhere.

## 29.4 Port Captured, Port Still Unusable

A port hex is under friendly control.

The port is not yet active under Scenario rules.

Do not use control as a substitute for port state.

## 29.5 Air Interdiction Delays the Reserve

Enemy reserve R could reach the threatened sector by Operational Movement.

Interdiction prevents that movement mode.

The reserve still exists.

Its combat factors did not change.

But it may now arrive too late.

## 29.6 Objective Captured, Victory Not Yet Secured

A side controls the required Objective at the end of its own actions.

The official victory check occurs only after the opponent's Player Turn.

The opponent recaptures the hex.

The victory condition was never satisfied at the formal check.

## 29.7 Local Battle Won, Operational Position Worsened

Attacker A wins, advances deeply, and leaves friendly B behind.

The supply route is now narrow.

Enemy reserve R threatens the flank.

The combat result was good.

The operational decision may have been poor.

# 30. How to Use “What Happened Historically” Without Turning It into a Script

Historical knowledge is useful.

It can tell you:

- which roads were important;
- why Caen mattered;
- why Cherbourg mattered;
- why German reserve timing mattered;
- how bocage changed the western campaign;
- why Falaise became a struggle over exits.

But history should not be used as a hidden extra rule.

Do not say:

> Historically the Germans counterattacked here, therefore the German player should do the same.

Instead ask:

> What operational conditions made that counterattack attractive or dangerous?

Do not say:

> Cobra happened here, therefore the Allied player must attack here.

Instead ask:

> What conditions allowed a concentrated breakthrough to succeed?

The best use of history is not to copy decisions.

It is to understand the problems behind those decisions.

# Part Five — Design Notes, Sources, and Unresolved Data

# 31. Design Notes — Why the Scenario Is Shaped This Way

## 31.1 Why One Game Turn Represents Three Days

The campaign contains changes that are too slow for tactical turns and too fast for weekly turns.

A three-day Game Turn is intended to make:

- reserve timing;
- reinforcement;
- supply;
- multi-step offensives;
- exploitation;

matter without forcing the player to manage each day separately.

## 31.2 Why Roughly 10 Kilometers per Hex

The map scale must be broad enough to represent the full Normandy operational area while still making:

- roads;
- major terrain;
- ports;
- important inland corridors;
- reserve distances;

meaningful.

The exact map remains a future implementation task.

## 31.3 Why Regiment / Brigade Scale Is Primary

The game needs formations large enough to represent operational maneuver while still showing meaningful front structure.

Divisions are important as higher groupings.

Battalions should appear independently only when they have real operational significance.

Exact formation rosters remain Game Data.

## 31.4 Why the German Player Sets Up First

The German player must commit to a defense under uncertainty.

This creates a real setup problem instead of allowing perfect reaction to a known Allied plan.

## 31.5 Why German Setup Is Visible to the Allied Player

The Allied player historically possessed planning, reconnaissance, intelligence, and the initiative to choose the invasion plan.

The Scenario does not simulate every intelligence source separately.

Visible German setup gives the Allied player useful information while still constraining the response through Scenario-defined invasion rules.

## 31.6 Why the Initial Invasion Happens Inside Game Turn 1

The invasion should interact with:

- Special Operations timing;
- the first Supply Checkpoint;
- movement;
- combat;
- the first German response.

Treating the invasion as a detached mini-game would weaken those connections.

## 31.7 Why the Two Victory Checks Occur at Different Player-Turn Ends

The timing forces each side to consider whether a position can survive the opponent's opportunity to act.

It prevents an objective from becoming decisive merely because it was occupied briefly at a convenient moment.

## 31.8 Why There Is No Universal Fixed End Date

A fixed final date can be useful in some games.

The current Scenario design instead emphasizes operational resolution through objective control, concession, or mutual draw.

This avoids declaring a campaign finished only because a calendar threshold was reached while meaningful operational play remains.

# 32. What We Learn from Earlier Wargames

## 32.1 What Is Useful to Study

Earlier Normandy wargames and rulebooks are valuable as design evidence for questions such as:

- what players need to know before setup;
- how scenario information is separated from common rules;
- how examples of play are presented;
- how history and player guidance can be integrated;
- how reference material helps table use.

## 32.2 What We Do Not Copy

We do not copy:

- proprietary rule text;
- setup tables;
- unit factors;
- counter rosters;
- map data;
- scenario values;
- copyrighted scenario content.

Prior designs are evidence, not authority.

## 32.3 The Role of Avalon Hill D-Day 1977

Avalon Hill's 1977 D-Day design is a major reference point for the project.

It is not the answer.

The Scenario may learn from the problems that earlier designs chose to emphasize, while building its own rules architecture and data.

## 32.4 The Scenario Book Itself Is Also a Design Problem

Modern wargame publishing often separates:

- rules;
- playbooks;
- scenario books;
- examples;
- references.

That separation matters.

A Scenario Book should not be a thin setup sheet if the campaign itself requires historical and operational explanation.

The accepted B2/B3 model therefore treats the Scenario Book as a substantial player-facing book.
