# Operational Wargame Rules Companion

**Chapters 1–15 Reader-Value Expansion v0.4 / Working Draft**

This Companion is not a shortened copy of the Player Rulebook.

Its purpose is to teach players **what the rules represent, why they are structured this way, how systems interact, what mistakes players commonly make, and how rules become operational decisions on the map**.

The Player Rulebook remains the formal user-facing rules source. Use it when an exact condition, timing point, permission, or restriction matters.

Think of the three user-facing books this way:

- **Player Rulebook:** resolve the procedure correctly.
- **Scenario Book:** understand this campaign, its setup, its objectives, and its historical-operational problem.
- **Rules Companion:** understand why the system behaves as it does and how to think with it.

---

# 1. How to Learn the System

Operational wargames become difficult when players try to memorize isolated rules.

In actual play, one action often touches several systems at once.

Moving one formation forward may require you to think about:

- the current Step;
- Movement Points;
- enemy Zones of Control;
- stacking;
- supply;
- later combat;
- the next enemy response;
- the victory objective you are actually trying to achieve.

A useful way to organize the entire system is around four questions.

1. **What time is it?**  
   Pre-game, which Player Turn, and which Step?

2. **Where can this formation act?**  
   Movement, Zones of Control, stacking, combat, and Special Operations.

3. **Can this formation keep operating?**  
   Supply, isolation, reinforcement, replacement, and Entry Sources.

4. **Why am I doing this?**  
   Victory Conditions and Game End Conditions.

These four questions are more useful than memorizing chapter numbers.

## 1.1 Beginners Read the Current State

At first, it is enough to ask:

- Can this unit move now?
- Can it attack now?
- Is it supplied now?

That lets you play legally.

## 1.2 Experienced Players Build the Next State

With experience, the question changes.

You begin asking:

- Can I open this road before my next Supply Checkpoint?
- Can I force this retreat before resolving the next combat?
- Can I hold this Objective through the opponent's response?
- Can I move this reserve now without giving up another option?

Operational play is largely about **building the map state that you want to exist at the next important checkpoint**.

---

## 1.3 Read One Action Through Four Lenses

Suppose you are considering moving formation A forward along a road.

A new player often begins with:

> How many hexes can it move?

That is a legal-movement question, but it is only one part of the decision.

A more useful operational reading uses four lenses.

### Time

- Which Step is active?
- Is combat still to come?
- When is the next Supply Checkpoint?
- When is the next Victory Check?

### Position

- Will A enter an enemy Zone of Control?
- Will it remain connected to friendly formations?
- Does the road or bridge matter?
- Can an enemy reserve reach the new position?

### Continuity

- Can A remain supplied after moving?
- Can reinforcements or friendly reserves support it?
- Does A retain a useful retreat route?
- Is the move creating a position that can survive the next enemy turn?

### Purpose

- Does the move advance a Victory Condition?
- Does it secure a road or Entry Source?
- Does it force an enemy reserve to react?
- Or is it simply movement because movement is available?

The four-lens method turns a legal action into an operational decision.

## 1.4 When You Are Confused, Identify the Kind of Question First

You do not need to search the entire documentation set every time something feels unclear.

First classify the problem.

### “Is this legal?”

Use the Player Rulebook.

### “What does this campaign use?”

Use the Scenario Book.

### “Why does this rule work this way?”

Use this Companion.

### “I cannot find the number.”

Determine whether the missing value belongs to the common rules or to Scenario data.

This classification is often more useful than memorizing a page number.

## 1.5 You Do Not Need to Memorize Everything

Operational games contain too many interactions for perfect memory to be a sensible learning goal.

What matters more is recognizing:

- which system is probably relevant;
- what timing point matters;
- which source has formal authority;
- what kind of state needs to be checked.

If you can think:

> This looks like a Zone of Control issue.

or:

> This answer depends on the last Supply Checkpoint.

or:

> That value may be Scenario-defined rather than common.

then you already know where to look.

The goal of this Companion is not perfect recall.

It is **reliable use of the rules when a real board position asks a question**.

# 2. Scale and Abstraction

## 2.1 Operational Rather Than Tactical

The system does not ask which building an infantry company occupies or which direction each tank is facing.

It asks broader questions:

- Which sector receives strength?
- Which route must remain open?
- Where should the reserve stand?
- Which attack is worth losses?
- When should a formation disengage and redeploy?
- Where should limited air or special resources be committed?

The rules therefore model operational effects rather than every physical detail.

## 2.2 Abstraction Is Compression

A Zone of Control is not a literal weapons range.

It compresses several effects into one usable rule:

- enemy proximity;
- caution and deployment;
- inability to march freely past contact;
- operational friction caused by a nearby enemy formation.

The purpose of abstraction is not merely to omit reality.

It is to convert many real effects into a decision the player can actually use.

## 2.3 Ask What the Rule Is Trying to Preserve

When a rule feels simplified, do not begin with:

> Reality would be more complicated than this.

Instead ask:

> Which operational problem is this rule preserving?

A Zone of Control preserves the problem of enemy contact.

Supply preserves the problem of sustaining an advance.

Entry Sources preserve the problem of getting future forces into the theater.

Victory Objectives preserve the problem of deciding which locations matter to the campaign.

This way of reading makes the system easier to understand as a whole.

---

## 2.4 The Same Event Looks Different at Tactical and Operational Scale

Consider an attack across a river.

A tactical game might care about:

- which bridge is used;
- when engineers arrive;
- which company crosses first;
- where direct-fire weapons are placed;
- how long a crossing takes in minutes.

An operational game usually does not model every one of those actions separately.

Instead, it asks a larger question:

> Is it harder to apply combat power across this river than across open ground?

The rule compresses many lower-level difficulties into an operational consequence that the player can use.

This is not a claim that the smaller details did not matter historically.

It is a decision about **which level of command the player is being asked to perform**.

## 2.5 A Hex Is an Area, Not a Point

A hex may contain many real features:

- local roads;
- farms;
- villages;
- woods;
- slopes;
- minor streams;
- defensive positions.

The map presents the features that matter at the selected scale.

A player should therefore avoid reasoning from invisible tactical detail as though the hex were a precise physical point.

The relevant question is:

> Which conditions has the game chosen to preserve at operational scale?

## 2.6 A Counter Is More Than a Headcount

A formation counter is not a box containing a precise number of individual soldiers.

It represents an organized operational formation, including some combination of:

- manpower;
- equipment;
- command;
- transport;
- cohesion;
- ability to continue operating.

That is why a Step loss is not simply a percentage of men lost, and why supply or movement state can matter even when the printed combat strength has not changed.

## 2.7 More Special Rules Do Not Automatically Mean More Realism

A historical event can be important without requiring a dedicated subsystem.

Before adding a special rule, ask:

> Can the existing systems already create the important operational decision?

Movement, supply, control, reinforcement, combat, and timing can often represent a historical problem without a new exception.

A special rule is most valuable when the existing rules cannot preserve the decision that mattered.

Otherwise, detail can increase while understanding decreases.

# 3. Time: Player Turns, Steps, and Persistent State

## 3.1 Sequence Is Part of the Rule

The Player-Turn sequence is not just an organizational list.

It determines **when a state is checked and how long that state remains valid**.

Supply is the clearest example. A unit's supply state is determined at the side's Supply Checkpoint. A route opened later in the same Player Turn does not necessarily rewrite the state that was already established.

The sequence therefore creates planning across turns.

## 3.2 Pre-Game Is Not Game Turn 1

Game Setup may include deployment, observation, and pre-game commitment.

Those actions are not automatically Player Turns.

This distinction matters because Supply Checkpoints, combat permissions, and victory checks are tied to the formal turn structure.

## 3.3 The Map Can Change Before the State Changes

A road may be open now even though a unit is still Unsupplied.

A port may become usable now even though the current Supply Checkpoint has already passed.

This is not a contradiction.

It means the system distinguishes:

- **what the board looks like now**, and
- **which state is currently in force**.

## 3.4 Worked Example — The Road Is Open, but Supply Has Not Changed Yet

Formation P is Unsupplied at its Supply Checkpoint because an enemy position blocks the route.

Later in the turn, friendly formation Q moves and opens the road.

The map now shows a possible supply route.

P does not automatically become Supplied in the middle of the Player Turn.

The operational value of Q's move is that P may qualify for supply at the **next** relevant Supply Checkpoint.

The example links:

- sequence;
- supply state;
- movement;
- road control;
- planning across turns.

---

## 3.5 A Useful Mental Timeline

A Player Turn can be understood as a sequence of four ideas:

**check a state**  
↓  
**act under that state**  
↓  
**change the map**  
↓  
**build conditions for the next checkpoint**

This is the simplest way to understand persistent state.

The map can improve after a checkpoint without immediately rewriting the state established at that checkpoint.

Examples include:

- opening a supply road after supply was checked;
- making a port usable after the current supply state was established;
- capturing an Objective before the official Victory Check;
- clearing an Entry route after reinforcement conditions were already assessed.

The system separates “what the board looks like now” from “what state is in force now.”

## 3.6 Separate Immediate Map Changes from Later State Changes

Some changes take effect on the map at once.

Examples:

- a formation moves;
- a Zone of Control changes;
- a road becomes physically open;
- two formations become adjacent.

Other consequences matter at the next formal checkpoint.

Examples:

- future supply status;
- future reinforcement access;
- victory at a designated check time.

If these are mixed together, a player may incorrectly conclude:

> The road is open, therefore the unit must already be Supplied.

The road condition changed immediately.

The supply state may not change until the next check.

## 3.7 Worked Example — Same Map, Different Timing

Formation A moves into Objective O.

Now compare two cases.

### Case 1: Victory is checked immediately after the active side's actions

A may satisfy the condition as soon as the formal check occurs.

### Case 2: Victory is checked after the opponent's Player Turn

The opponent has time to counterattack and recapture O.

The map position at the moment of capture is identical.

The operational meaning is different because the official check happens at a different time.

This is why “Can I take it?” and “Can I hold it until the check?” are different questions.

## 3.8 Worked Example — A Port Becomes Usable After Supply Was Checked

Port P is unavailable at the Supply Checkpoint.

Later in the Player Turn, the conditions for making P usable are satisfied.

The correct interpretation is not:

> Recalculate supply retroactively.

It is:

> A new condition now exists that may matter at the next relevant Supply Checkpoint.

The port became useful.

The past checkpoint did not move.

## 3.9 Think Across Two Turns

A new player naturally tries to maximize the current Player Turn.

Operational play often becomes clearer when you think across two turns.

For example:

**Turn 1:** open the road  
↓  
**enemy response:** keep the road open  
↓  
**Turn 2 Supply Checkpoint:** restore supply if conditions are met  
↓  
**Turn 2:** resume the offensive

If you only evaluate Turn 1, it can look like the force “wasted a turn.”

If you evaluate the sequence, Turn 1 was preparation for Turn 2.

## 3.10 Common Timing Misreadings

### “If the board now satisfies the condition, the state changes immediately.”

Not always. Check when the state is determined.

### “Pre-game activity is part of Player Turn 1.”

Not automatically. Game Setup and formal Player Turns are distinct.

### “If I satisfy the Victory Condition for a moment, I win.”

The condition must exist at the official check time.

### “I can just recalculate everything from the current board.”

Persistent states may depend on an earlier checkpoint.

## 3.11 Advanced Reading — Plan Backward from the Next Checkpoint

Experienced players often think backward.

Instead of asking only:

> What can I do now?

they ask:

> What do I want the map to look like when the next important check occurs?

Timing rules are not administrative restrictions.

They are the framework that makes multi-turn planning possible.

# 4. Units, Steps, Stacking, and Control

## 4.1 What a Step Represents

A Step is not a precise headcount.

It compresses several forms of operational degradation:

- personnel losses;
- equipment loss;
- organizational disruption;
- reduced cohesion;
- reduced ability to continue fighting effectively.

A formation at reduced strength is therefore not simply “half as many soldiers.”

It is a formation with meaningfully lower operational effectiveness.

## 4.2 Why Stacking Is Not Just Counter Count

One counter does not always represent the same size formation as another.

A simple rule such as “three counters per hex” would treat a large formation and a small independent formation as though they consumed the same operational space.

Stacking demand allows the game to represent formation density without making counter count itself the scale.

## 4.3 Occupation and Control Are Different Ideas

A formation can occupy a hex.

A side can control a hex.

A formation can also move through a location without necessarily creating the same long-term control state that matters for victory or supply.

Whenever a rule refers to an Objective, route, or Entry Source, check whether it cares about:

- presence;
- occupation;
- control.

Do not treat those words as interchangeable.

## 4.4 A Reduced Step Is Not a Literal Percentage

A two-Step unit that loses one Step has not necessarily lost exactly half its personnel.

The Step system is an operational state model, not a bookkeeping model.

Replacement should be understood in the same way: restoring a Step may represent replacement personnel, recovered equipment, reorganization, rest, and restored effectiveness.

## 4.5 Worked Example — One Large Formation versus Two Small Ones

Hex A contains one large formation.

Hex B contains two smaller formations.

Counter count says Hex B contains “more units.”

Stacking demand may say the opposite.

The point is simple:

**counter count is presentation; stacking demand is operational density.**

---

## 4.6 Presence, Occupation, and Control Are Not the Same

A formation can pass through a hex without every rule treating that hex as controlled in the same way.

A side can also retain control of a location without a unit physically occupying it, depending on the relevant rule.

When a Victory Condition, Supply rule, or Entry rule refers to a location, read the exact concept it requires.

Ask:

- Must a friendly unit be present?
- Must the hex be occupied?
- Is side control sufficient?

Treating those words as interchangeable creates subtle but important errors.

## 4.7 A Reduced Formation Can Still Have Positional Value

Formation A loses a Step.

Its combat effectiveness is lower.

But if A still occupies a key road junction or blocks an enemy route, it may continue to provide major operational value.

It can still:

- interfere with enemy movement;
- help hold a line;
- preserve a road;
- delay access to an Objective;
- force the enemy to spend time dealing with it.

Reduced strength does not mean zero usefulness.

## 4.8 Worked Example — Concentrate or Cover More Ground?

Formations A and B can stack in the same important hex.

Doing so creates a strong local position.

But the second sector then becomes thinner.

This is the operational cost of concentration.

Stacking is not only a limit that prevents too many counters from occupying one hex.

It creates a recurring choice between:

- strength in one place; and
- presence across more places.

## 4.9 Common Unit-Level Misreadings

### “The unit with the larger printed value is always more important.”

Position can matter more than raw strength.

### “A reduced formation should always withdraw.”

Not necessarily. Its location may still be strategically valuable.

### “More counters in a hex means a stronger stack.”

Check stacking demand, Steps, and actual formation state.

The central habit is to look beyond the counter and ask:

> What function is this formation performing on the map?

# 5. Movement and Zones of Control

**Abbreviations used in this chapter: Zone of Control, Movement Points.**

## 5.1 Movement Points Are Not Kilometers

Movement Points represent usable mobility during the current Player Turn.

Terrain, roads, rivers, and enemy contact can all change how much that mobility buys.

Two formations may travel the same physical distance while spending very different amounts of Movement Points.

## 5.2 Why Entering Enemy Zone of Control Ends Regular Movement

The rule prevents a formation from treating enemy contact as though it were empty terrain.

Once a formation enters an enemy Zone of Control, it cannot simply use remaining Movement Points to continue marching through the enemy's operational presence.

This preserves the importance of contact without requiring tactical engagement rules for every kilometer.

## 5.3 Remaining Movement Points Does Not Guarantee Permission to Move

This distinction prevents many mistakes.

Movement Points are a **cost resource**.

Zone of Control and other conditions are **permission constraints**.

Having enough cost capacity does not mean the action remains legal.

## 5.4 Operational Movement Is a Different Mode

Operational Movement is not “Regular Movement, but better.”

It represents rapid redeployment away from immediate combat.

That greater mobility comes with restrictions involving enemy contact, attack, or other conditions.

Use it when the operational question is:

> How do I move this reserve to another sector?

not:

> How do I get extra movement and still fight normally?

## 5.5 Roads, Major Rivers, and Bridges

A road does not erase every terrain rule.

A road may reduce movement cost without making otherwise prohibited terrain legal.

A bridge may remove an additional crossing cost without erasing every combat disadvantage associated with attacking across a Major River.

Movement effects and combat effects are separate.

## 5.6 Worked Example — The Road Continues, but the Unit Stops

Formation A moves efficiently along a road and enters an enemy Zone of Control with Movement Points remaining.

The next hex is also on the same road.

A still stops.

The cheap road cost does not override the contact rule.

## 5.7 Worked Example — Sending a Reserve by Operational Movement

Reserve R can reach the threatened sector by Operational Movement this turn.

If using that mode also prevents R from attacking immediately, the decision becomes:

- arrive sooner but not fight now; or
- arrive later with more tactical freedom.

The rule creates an operational tradeoff rather than simply granting bonus movement.

## 5.8 Advanced Reading — Maximum Movement Is Not Maximum Value

A formation does not become more useful simply because it uses every Movement Points.

Moving too far can:

- stretch supply;
- expose a flank;
- separate the formation from friendly support;
- enter enemy reserve range;
- narrow its retreat route.

Sometimes the strongest move is to stop early.

---

## 5.9 Regular Movement and Operational Movement Serve Different Jobs

It is useful to compare them conceptually.

| Question | Regular Movement | Operational Movement |
|---|---|---|
| Primary purpose | maneuver in or near the active front | larger redeployment across the operational area |
| Enemy contact | expected as part of frontline movement | strongly constrained |
| Immediate combat role | normally compatible with frontline action | may be restricted |
| Main decision | how far to move and where to stop | which sector should receive the reserve |

Exact conditions remain in the Player Rulebook.

The key point is that Operational Movement is not simply “Regular Movement with a larger allowance.”

It is a different operational mode.

## 5.10 Before and After — What One Road Can Change

### Before

Reserve R is far from the threatened sector and must cross difficult terrain.

### After

A major road is secured.

R has not become stronger.

What changed is:

- how quickly it can reach the front;
- how many sectors it can realistically support;
- how many options remain available next turn.

A road can therefore be valuable because it increases **choice**, not because it directly adds combat strength.

## 5.11 Read Rivers and Bridges as Two Separate Questions

When you see a river or bridge, ask two questions.

1. **What does this mean for movement?**
2. **What does this mean for combat?**

A bridge may reduce or remove a movement penalty without erasing every combat consequence of attacking across a Major River.

Do not compress every river effect into:

> bridge present = river irrelevant.

Movement and combat are distinct systems.

## 5.12 Worked Example — Three Reasons to Stop Before Maximum Movement

Formation A can legally move one more hex.

Why might it stop?

### Reason 1: Supply

The deeper position may be vulnerable at the next Supply Checkpoint.

### Reason 2: Enemy Reserve

The extra hex may bring A into the enemy reserve's effective response area.

### Reason 3: Friendly Connection

Advancing may open a gap between A and friendly formation B.

In all three cases, the formation **can** move.

The question is whether it **should** move.

## 5.13 Worked Example — Reserve Position Before Reserve Commitment

Reserve R can stand in a central location from which it can support either the northern or southern sector.

Moving it north now improves northern response time.

It also weakens the south before the enemy has committed.

Reserve placement is therefore not only about where the formation will go.

It is about **which destinations remain available**.

## 5.14 A Zone of Control Is Not a Physical Wall

A Zone of Control should not be imagined as an invisible barrier of exact weapon range.

It represents the operational consequence of nearby enemy presence.

The important question is not:

> How many kilometers can that unit fire?

It is:

> Can my formation move past this enemy presence as though the enemy were not there?

That is the abstraction the rule preserves.

## 5.15 Common Movement Misreadings

### “I still have Movement Points, so I may continue.”

Permission conditions still matter.

### “A road lets me ignore an enemy Zone of Control.”

A road can change movement cost without erasing enemy contact.

### “Operational Movement is the superior form of movement.”

No. It serves a different purpose and carries different restrictions.

## 5.16 Advanced Reading — Movement Can Reduce the Opponent's Choices

The best move is not always the shortest route toward an Objective.

A formation moved to a road junction may force the opponent to decide:

- defend north or south;
- commit a reserve early;
- protect a supply route;
- abandon another sector.

Movement can therefore shape the opponent's future decision space.

You are not only moving your own pieces.

You are also changing which moves the opponent can safely make.

# 6. Combat

## 6.1 Why Combat Is Resolved One at a Time

Combat order matters because one combat can alter the conditions for another.

A defender may retreat.

An attacker may advance after combat.

A Zone of Control may move.

A road may open.

A later attack may no longer be necessary.

Resolving combat sequentially turns combat order into player judgment.

## 6.2 Combat Band Logic

The system first establishes the relative force relationship and then modifies the situation through shifts.

This separates:

- basic force relationship; and
- battlefield conditions such as terrain, support, or other applicable factors.

The distinction is easier to reason about than one enormous table that hides every influence inside the initial ratio.

## 6.3 Worked Combat Example

Attacking formations A and B attack defending formation X.

1. Confirm that A and B may participate.
2. Check their current Steps and supply states.
3. Total relevant attack and defense strength.
4. Determine the base Combat Band.
5. Apply valid shifts such as terrain or support.
6. Determine the final Band.
7. Roll the die.
8. Apply losses.
9. Resolve retreat if required.
10. Resolve Post-Combat Advance if permitted.
11. Only then choose the next combat.

The combat is not finished when the loss result appears.

Position change is part of the combat result.

## 6.4 Terrain and Fortifications

Terrain should be read as a condition that changes how effectively force can be applied.

Fortifications are treated within the common Combat System rather than creating a separate siege game.

The important distinction is that movement effects, combat effects, and structure state are not automatically the same rule.

## 6.5 Do Not Choose Attacks Only by Odds

A favorable attack may still be a poor operational decision.

Before attacking, ask:

- What route opens if the defender retreats?
- Where can the defender retreat?
- Can I exploit the result?
- Can I remain supplied afterward?
- Does this help the victory objective?

## 6.6 Worked Example — The Weaker Enemy Is the Better Target

Enemy X is stronger but stands away from any important route.

Enemy Y is weaker and holds the key road junction.

Destroying or displacing Y may:

- open supply;
- allow reserves through;
- create a later approach to an Objective.

The operationally important target may therefore be the weaker formation.

---

## 6.7 Three Operational Purposes of Combat

It helps to classify an attack by what you actually want from it.

### Inflict Loss

Reduce enemy combat capability.

### Change Position

Force a retreat, open a road, expose a bridge, uncover an Objective, or create space for advance.

### Force a Response

Make the opponent commit a reserve, air resource, or other capability to this sector.

One combat can do all three.

But many attacks are valuable because of only one of them.

## 6.8 Worked Example — Combat Order Changes the Operation

Enemy X holds the entrance to an important road.

Enemy Y stands behind it.

You can attack both.

### Attack X first

If X retreats, the road opens and the local geometry changes before you decide what to do about Y.

### Attack Y first

You may damage Y, but X still blocks the route.

The correct order depends on the operational purpose.

The important habit is to give the sequence a reason.

## 6.9 Worked Example — Not Attacking Can Be the Stronger Choice

Enemy X occupies strong defensive terrain.

A frontal attack is legal but expensive.

A different maneuver can threaten the road behind X.

If that threat eventually forces X to move or weakens the wider enemy position, bypassing the battle may be more valuable than winning it directly.

Understanding combat includes understanding when combat is unnecessary.

## 6.10 Terrain Changes the Choice of Attack, Not Just the Modifier

It is easy to reduce terrain to:

> this gives the defender a favorable shift.

A stronger operational reading asks:

- Can I attack from another direction?
- Can part of the attack avoid crossing the Major River?
- Can I bypass the position?
- Can another combat change the situation first?

Terrain does not only affect the result.

It can determine **which operation is worth attempting at all**.

## 6.11 Loss and Position Are Separate Measures

A defender can suffer heavy loss and still hold the key road.

A defender can also take little loss but retreat from a location that makes the entire line unstable.

After every combat, evaluate:

1. losses;
2. position;
3. what actions are now possible.

This prevents the die result from becoming the only measure of success.

## 6.12 Worked Example — A Small Fight Creates a Large Opening

Enemy Y is weak but controls road junction J.

Forcing Y away may allow:

- a reserve to move forward;
- a supply route to shorten;
- a later attack from two directions.

The battle is small.

The map change is large.

## 6.13 Common Combat Misreadings

### “Resolve the best odds first.”

Resolve the combat that best supports the operation.

### “If an attack is favorable, I should make it.”

Every combat consumes position, risk, and possibly scarce support.

### “The result is mainly the loss result.”

Retreat and Post-Combat Advance may matter more.

### “The strongest enemy is the most important target.”

Location can make a weaker enemy more important.

## 6.14 Advanced Reading — Combat as Map Editing

At operational scale, combat is a way of changing the map.

It changes:

- enemy location;
- friendly location;
- Zones of Control;
- roads;
- supply;
- reserve requirements.

The die roll helps determine how much of that map change you achieve.

That is a more useful way to understand combat than treating it as a separate mini-game about casualties.

# 7. Retreat and Post-Combat Advance

## 7.1 Retreat Changes the Map

Retreat is not merely a penalty measured in hexes.

It can:

- open a road;
- expose a flank;
- change Zone of Control coverage;
- separate friendly formations;
- uncover an Objective.

A positional result can be more important than a Step loss.

## 7.2 Post-Combat Advance Converts Combat into Maneuver

Post-Combat Advance allows the attacker to use space created by combat immediately.

It is not free ordinary movement.

It is the mechanism that turns a local combat result into operational movement.

## 7.3 Worked Example — Win the Combat, Decline the Advance

Attacker A forces defender X to retreat.

A may advance.

The advance hex, however:

- stretches supply;
- lies within enemy reserve reach;
- separates A from friendly B;
- leaves only one practical route back.

A can legally advance and still choose not to.

A permission is not an obligation.

## 7.4 Advanced Reading

Every combat should be read twice:

1. What losses occurred?
2. What changed on the map?

The second question often matters more.

---

## 7.5 Retreat Changes the Shape of the Front

A one-hex retreat can have effects far larger than one hex.

It may:

- expose the flank of another formation;
- uncover a road junction;
- break a continuous Zone of Control pattern;
- create a Post-Combat Advance route;
- threaten a supply line.

Short retreat distance does not imply small operational effect.

## 7.6 Worked Example — No Step Loss, but the Position Collapses

Defender X takes no Step loss.

The combat result still forces X to retreat.

The exit from an important bridge is now empty.

Attacker A advances into the space.

A later defender, Y, is now exposed from a new direction.

The loss record says the defense suffered little damage.

The map says the front may be beginning to fail.

## 7.7 Worked Example — Declining Advance to Preserve the Line

Attacker A may advance after combat.

If it advances, a gap opens between A and friendly B.

Enemy reserve R can threaten that gap next turn.

By remaining in place, A preserves a connected line.

Declining Post-Combat Advance can therefore be a defensive preparation for the enemy Player Turn.

## 7.8 Look at Retreat Routes Before You Attack

Before rolling the combat, ask:

> Where can the defender go if forced to retreat?

Another combat may be able to narrow those routes first.

This does not require a separate encirclement mini-game.

It is simply the interaction of sequential combat and changing map state.

## 7.9 Advanced Reading — Advance Is the Next Risk You Choose

Post-Combat Advance accelerates exploitation.

It also lengthens the front.

Before advancing, compare:

- the position gained;
- the friendly connection lost;
- supply exposure;
- enemy reserve reach;
- retreat options.

Advance is not merely a reward for success.

It is the choice of what risk to accept next.

# 8. Supply

## 8.1 Supply Is Not Rear-Area Administration

Supply affects where an army can continue operating.

It helps determine:

- how far to advance;
- which roads matter;
- where to pause;
- which formations can remain forward;
- which routes must be protected.

Supply is therefore a planning system, not merely a status check.

## 8.2 Why Supply Is Checked at a Checkpoint

Recalculating supply after every move and combat would create bookkeeping without necessarily creating better operational decisions.

A checkpoint creates a stable state long enough for the player to plan around it.

That is why opening a route later in the turn can matter greatly without instantly rewriting the current supply state.

## 8.3 Unsupplied Is Not the Same as Isolated

A formation can fail to receive supply without being completely cut off from the friendly operational network.

Isolation represents a more severe form of disconnection.

The distinction allows the game to separate temporary supply difficulty from operational encirclement.

## 8.4 Ports and Supply

Capturing a port does not automatically mean that it is functioning as a full Supply Source.

Port state, repair, sea access, and capacity may all be Scenario-defined.

A port that becomes usable after the current Supply Checkpoint may be strategically important without retroactively changing a supply check that has already occurred.

## 8.5 Worked Example — The Port Opens Too Late for This Turn

Port P is unavailable at the Supply Checkpoint.

Later in the Player Turn, its conditions are satisfied and it becomes usable.

The current supply state does not rewind.

The value of the port is that it can support future supply planning.

## 8.6 Worked Example — A Successful Advance That Goes Too Far

Formations A and B win a combat and can advance deeply along a road.

If they advance to the maximum possible position, the route behind them becomes long and vulnerable.

The player may decide to stop short, preserve connection, and attack again next turn.

The combat was successful.

The strongest operational use of that success may still be a limited advance.

## 8.7 Advanced Reading

Beginners ask:

> Am I supplied now?

Experienced players ask:

> What must the map look like at my next Supply Checkpoint?

That shift in thinking is one of the most important steps in learning operational play.

---

## 8.8 A Supply Line Is Really a Supply Structure

Thinking of supply as a single line can be misleading.

On the map, supply depends on a structure made from several elements:

- Supply Sources;
- roads;
- bridges;
- control;
- enemy interference;
- the position of forward formations.

A weak point anywhere in that structure can affect several formations.

That is why supply warfare can involve attacking the support system rather than the frontline formation itself.

## 8.9 Worked Example — One Road Supports Three Formations

Forward formations A, B, and C all depend on road J.

Attacking A directly affects A.

Severing J may create a supply problem for all three at the next relevant check.

J does not need to be a Victory Objective to be operationally important.

## 8.10 Distinguish Unsupplied from Isolated on the Map

### Unsupplied

The formation currently fails the supply conditions.

It may still have meaningful friendly connection and a realistic prospect of recovery.

### Isolated

The formation is more severely cut off from the friendly operational network.

The formal definitions belong to the Player Rulebook.

The conceptual distinction matters because temporary supply difficulty and operational encirclement create different kinds of pressure.

## 8.11 Read a Port in Three Stages

When evaluating a port, separate:

1. **friendly control of the location;**
2. **the port becoming usable;**
3. **the Scenario-defined capacity and conditions under which it can actually support supply.**

These stages do not have to occur at the same moment.

Capturing the map location does not automatically solve the logistics problem.

## 8.12 Worked Example — Winning Faster Makes Supply Worse

Formations A, B, and C win several combats and advance rapidly.

The enemy is withdrawing.

The offensive appears highly successful.

But the front is now farther from the Supply Source.

Only one road supports the advance.

A small enemy formation is approaching that road.

The question becomes:

> Should the force continue exploiting success, or pause before the next Supply Checkpoint?

Operational success can create a new logistics problem precisely because it succeeded.

## 8.13 Worked Example — Attack the Enemy or Cut the Road?

You have two possible operations.

### Plan A

Attack enemy main formation X and try to inflict major loss.

### Plan B

Displace smaller formation Y and take road J.

Plan A may look better on the Combat Band.

Plan B may threaten the enemy supply structure.

The better choice depends on:

- the next Supply Checkpoint;
- enemy reserve position;
- Victory Conditions;
- what the road enables next turn.

Supply creates important targets that are not themselves powerful formations.

## 8.14 Two-Turn Worked Example — Restore Supply Before Resuming the Offensive

### Turn 1 begins

Forward formation P is Unsupplied.

Enemy Y blocks the important road.

### Turn 1 actions

Friendly Q forces Y away and opens the road.

P does not instantly become Supplied merely because the map improved.

The player therefore avoids an unnecessary attack with P and instead protects the restored route.

### Enemy response

The enemy attempts to cut the road again.

Q and rear-area formation R keep the route open.

### Turn 2 Supply Checkpoint

If all formal conditions are satisfied, P can now be reassessed under the supply rules.

### Turn 2 operations

P can participate in the renewed offensive from a better state.

The purpose of Turn 1 was not to maximize enemy loss.

It was to create the conditions for Turn 2.

## 8.15 A Rear-Area Formation Can Be Doing Essential Work

Formation R may be sitting on a road junction far from the most dramatic combat.

If R is protecting:

- the supply route;
- reinforcement access;
- the connection between front and rear;

then it is directly supporting the offensive.

Supply rules give operational value to positions that would otherwise look inactive.

## 8.16 Common Supply Misreadings

### “I am Supplied now, so deep advance is safe.”

Look at the next Supply Checkpoint.

### “The road reopened, so the state changes immediately.”

Check the timing rule.

### “We captured the port, so logistics are solved.”

Check port state, capacity, and timing.

### “An Unsupplied formation must always be rescued.”

Compare the rescue cost with the main operational purpose.

## 8.17 Advanced Reading — Supply Controls Campaign Tempo

Supply does more than switch formations between “can fight” and “cannot fight.”

Stable supply allows a force to:

- sustain offensive tempo;
- move reserves forward;
- support damaged formations;
- continue exploiting success.

Unstable supply may force a player to:

- pause;
- shorten the line;
- secure roads;
- repair access;
- reorganize.

Supply is therefore one of the systems that determines **how fast the campaign can safely move**.

# 9. Reinforcements and Replacements

## 9.1 Available Does Not Mean Entered

A reinforcement can become Available without appearing immediately on the map.

It still depends on:

- an Entry Source;
- capacity;
- Scenario conditions;
- possible pressure on the entry area.

Availability and Entry are different states.

## 9.2 Entry Sources Are Operational Infrastructure

An Entry Source is not simply a spawn point.

It is a route through which future combat power becomes present combat power.

Protecting an Entry Source can therefore matter even when no reinforcement is arriving this turn.

## 9.3 Replacement Is Not Automatic Reconstitution

Replacement resources restore eligible losses.

They do not automatically recreate every eliminated formation.

Reconstitution is a larger organizational problem and requires explicit permission when the Scenario allows it.

## 9.4 Worked Example — The Reinforcement Exists but Cannot Enter Yet

Reinforcement R becomes Available.

The intended Entry Source is already at capacity.

R is not automatically placed somewhere convenient.

The player must use an authorized alternative if one exists or wait.

The operational lesson is:

**future strength depends on access, not only schedule.**

---

## 9.5 Think of Reinforcement in Three Stages

A reinforcement is easier to understand when separated into three stages.

1. **It becomes Available.**
2. **An authorized Entry Source is selected.**
3. **Capacity and other conditions allow it to enter the map.**

These stages prevent the common mistake:

> The unit is scheduled this turn, therefore I may place it wherever convenient.

Availability is permission to begin the entry process, not unrestricted placement.

## 9.6 Worked Example — Defending an Entry Source Before the Reinforcement Arrives

Important reinforcements are expected through Entry Source E next turn.

A small enemy formation is approaching E.

That enemy is not currently threatening the main front.

But if it interferes with E, the future reinforcement plan may become much worse.

Using rear-area formation R to protect E can therefore be an operationally important action.

Protecting future strength is still part of present strategy.

## 9.7 Worked Example — Do Not Automatically Send the New Unit to the Front

Reinforcement R enters the map.

Sending it immediately to the main attack would increase frontline power.

But road junction J is undefended.

If J is lost, several friendly formations may face movement or supply problems.

R may be more valuable protecting the operational structure than adding one more counter to the attack.

## 9.8 Replacement and Reconstitution Solve Different Problems

Replacing losses in an existing formation is not the same as recreating an eliminated formation.

Reconstitution may imply rebuilding:

- command structure;
- equipment;
- cohesion;
- organizational identity.

That is why it requires explicit permission when allowed.

Do not assume general Replacement Points automatically recreate eliminated units.

## 9.9 Advanced Reading — Reinforcement Is Scheduled Future Power

A reinforcement not yet on the map can still influence current decisions.

If you know strength is arriving next turn, you can prepare by:

- keeping a road open;
- protecting an Entry Source;
- creating reserve space;
- avoiding unnecessary current losses.

The reinforcement schedule is therefore not merely future bookkeeping.

It is information that should influence today's plan.

# 10. Tactical Air and Heavy Breakthrough Bombardment

**Abbreviations used in this chapter: Tactical Air Points, Heavy Breakthrough Bombardment.**

## 10.1 Why Aircraft Are Represented as Resources

At operational scale, the important question is not which individual aircraft counter flies across which hex.

The important question is:

> Where should limited air power be committed?

Tactical Air Points represent that allocation problem.

## 10.2 Combat Support versus Operational Interdiction

Air support can improve a current combat.

Operational Interdiction can instead make enemy redeployment more difficult.

That creates a choice between:

- making one fight stronger now; or
- shaping the next several hexes and turns.

## 10.3 Why Heavy Breakthrough Bombardment Is Separate

Heavy Breakthrough Bombardment represents a prepared, exceptional concentration of support rather than simply “spend more Tactical Air Points.”

Keeping it separate preserves the idea that some breakthrough support is a scarce operational opportunity.

## 10.4 Worked Example — Help the Attack or Delay the Reserve

Attack A would benefit from Tactical Air Points.

Enemy reserve R is also preparing to move toward the threatened sector.

Using Tactical Air Points on Attack A may improve the immediate result.

Using air resources for Operational Interdiction may leave Attack A weaker but delay R long enough for a breakthrough to develop.

The correct choice depends on the operational objective.

## 10.5 Advanced Reading

Air power is best understood as a resource that can buy:

- combat advantage;
- time;
- reduced enemy mobility.

The last two are often less visible but operationally decisive.

---

## 10.6 Think of Air Power as Having Three Uses

Air resources can serve three broad operational jobs.

### Strengthen the current combat

Improve the immediate local battle.

### Restrict enemy movement

Delay or complicate reserve response and redeployment.

### Remain unspent for a more decisive moment

This third option matters.

A resource is not valuable because it was spent.

It is valuable because it was spent when its effect mattered most.

## 10.7 Worked Example — The Value of Delaying a Reserve by One Turn

Enemy reserve R can reach a vulnerable sector next turn.

Operational Interdiction may delay that movement.

Even without inflicting losses on R, one turn of delay may let your side:

- expand a Beachhead;
- secure a road;
- bring in reinforcements;
- stabilize supply;
- occupy a key position before R arrives.

Time can be more valuable than damage.

## 10.8 Worked Example — Win Without Spending the Air Resource

Attack A is already favorable.

Air support would make it even safer.

Elsewhere, however, a potential breakthrough may appear next turn.

The player can choose to keep the air resource unspent.

The goal is not to maximize the success probability of every individual battle.

It is to maximize the value of the scarce resource across the operation.

## 10.9 How to Read Heavy Breakthrough Bombardment

Heavy Breakthrough Bombardment should not be understood as simply “a larger pile of Tactical Air Points.”

It represents a prepared and comparatively scarce concentration intended for a breakthrough context.

Its meaning includes:

- preparation;
- concentration;
- limited opportunity;
- operational timing.

It is therefore not a generic substitute when ordinary air resources are insufficient.

## 10.10 Common Air-Power Misreadings

### “Air power is mainly for combat support.”

Operational Interdiction can be equally important.

### “If I have the resource, I should spend it.”

Future timing matters.

### “If the enemy takes no losses, the air effort achieved little.”

Delaying movement can change an entire turn.

## 10.11 Advanced Reading — Air Power Creates Unequal Time

One way to understand air power is that your side may continue operating on schedule while the enemy is delayed.

That difference in time can create temporary local superiority even when total forces are similar.

Air resources can therefore change **when combat power arrives**, not only how strong it is once present.

# 11. Airborne Operations

## 11.1 Position Now, Connection Later

Airborne forces can reach positions before ordinary ground movement can.

That advantage comes with a risk: they are easier to isolate.

An airborne operation therefore purchases **early position** and accepts a later requirement to create ground connection.

## 11.2 Reference Distance

Airborne placement is bounded by Scenario-defined reference rules.

That prevents airborne forces from becoming unrestricted teleportation.

An initial operation may require a special reference hex when ordinary friendly ground references do not yet exist.

## 11.3 Worked Example — Deep Effect versus Safe Link-Up

Location A is deep and can disrupt an important enemy route.

Location B is less disruptive but easier for friendly ground forces to reach.

A offers higher effect and higher isolation risk.

B offers lower effect and better survivability.

The decision is an operational tradeoff, not a puzzle with one universal answer.

---

## 11.4 Three Common Purposes of an Airborne Operation

Airborne placement can serve different operational purposes.

### Seize an important location early

A bridge, route, or Objective can be occupied before ordinary ground movement could reach it.

### Disrupt enemy movement

The airborne force can threaten roads or force enemy reserves to react.

### Support the wider ground operation

Its position can help amphibious or ground forces create a faster link-up.

The best drop location depends on which of these purposes matters most.

## 11.5 Worked Example — Deep Drop versus Reliable Link-Up

Location A is deep in the enemy rear and threatens an important route.

Location B is less disruptive but easier for friendly ground forces to reach.

A offers greater immediate effect and greater isolation risk.

B offers less dramatic impact but a better chance of becoming part of the sustained ground campaign.

There is no universal answer.

The player is choosing between effect and survivability.

## 11.6 Do Not Judge Airborne Success Only by Survival

An airborne force can suffer loss and still accomplish something important if it:

- delays an enemy reserve;
- blocks a road temporarily;
- holds a location long enough for another operation to succeed.

Conversely, a force that survives untouched but has little influence on the operation may have achieved less.

The operational question is:

> What changed because the force was there?

## 11.7 Why an Initial Reference-Hex Exception Exists

At the beginning of a Scenario, the normal friendly ground reference required for Airborne Operations may not yet exist.

A Scenario-defined initial reference hex solves that starting-state problem.

It does not create unrestricted airborne placement.

It is a bounded exception for a condition that exists specifically before the ground campaign is established.

# 12. Amphibious Operations

## 12.1 Lift Is a Force-Allocation Problem

An amphibious force cannot necessarily place every formation ashore at once.

Lift capacity and formation demand turn the opening into a force-allocation decision.

## 12.2 Opposed Assaults

An opposed landing uses the common Combat System as much as possible, with only the necessary differences for assaulting from the sea.

The design goal is not to create a separate tactical beach game.

It is to connect the landing directly to the operational campaign.

## 12.3 Why a Surviving Defender Cannot Simply Be Attacked Twice

If a defender survives the amphibious assault and could immediately be attacked again by ordinary combat in the same Combat Step, the same time interval would be used twice.

The protection is therefore a timing rule, not a faction-specific favor.

## 12.4 Worked Example — One Strong Formation or Two Flexible Ones

Lift capacity can carry either one large formation A or two smaller formations B and C.

A may create the strongest single assault.

B and C may create two positions, protect more frontage, or provide more flexibility after landing.

Lift is not merely a cap.

It is a way of deciding what shape the opening operation will have.

---

## 12.5 Read an Amphibious Operation in Three Stages

An amphibious operation becomes easier to understand when divided into three stages.

1. **Move forces from offshore into the assault.**
2. **Survive the landing and defeat or displace local resistance.**
3. **Expand inland and turn the landing into a continuing land campaign.**

Winning the first two stages does not guarantee the third.

A force can be ashore and still remain operationally trapped near the coast.

## 12.6 Worked Example — One Powerful Assault or a Broader Foothold?

Lift can carry either:

- one powerful formation A; or
- two smaller formations B and C.

A may create the strongest single assault.

B and C may:

- cover two exits;
- create a wider initial frontage;
- provide local reserve flexibility.

The best choice depends on the operation you want after the beach fight is over.

## 12.7 Read the Protection Against a Second Attack as a Timing Rule

Suppose a defender survives the amphibious assault.

If the same defender could immediately be attacked again by ordinary combat in the same Combat Step, the same time interval would effectively be used twice against one defending position.

The protection exists to preserve time consistency.

It is not primarily about favoring the attacker or defender.

## 12.8 Five Things to Check After the Landing Combat

After the landing succeeds, look at:

1. inland exits;
2. Beachhead width;
3. room for later reinforcement;
4. future supply;
5. enemy reserve approach routes.

This prevents the premature conclusion:

> We won the landing combat, therefore the invasion is secure.

## 12.9 Advanced Reading — Lift Capacity Is Opening Strategy

Lift capacity is not just a cap on how many counters can be placed.

The first formations selected determine:

- opening combat power;
- frontage;
- reserve flexibility;
- ability to expand inland.

The amphibious plan is therefore partly a force-composition decision made before the land campaign is fully established.

# 13. Beachheads

A Beachhead converts a successful landing into a continuing operational base.

Depending on Scenario rules, it may support:

- supply;
- reinforcement entry;
- continued operations inland.

A Beachhead is not automatically permanent.

Capacity, persistence, and loss conditions remain Scenario-defined.

## 13.1 Worked Example — Successful Landing, Weak Beachhead

A landing succeeds and formation A survives ashore.

The surrounding area remains narrow, an inland exit is blocked, and enemy counterattack threatens the access route.

The landing itself succeeded.

The operational base is still fragile.

The player must distinguish:

**getting ashore**
from
**building a position that can sustain a campaign.**

---

## 13.2 Three Operational Jobs of a Beachhead

A Beachhead can serve several functions, depending on Scenario rules.

### Supply access

It helps sustain forces already ashore.

### Reinforcement access

It allows later forces to enter the theater.

### Operational base

It provides the rear area from which the land campaign expands inland.

Its value should therefore not be measured by only one of those functions.

## 13.3 Narrow and Wide Beachheads Create Different Problems

A narrow Beachhead may be easier to defend locally.

But it can also produce:

- congestion;
- too few inland exits;
- concentration of vulnerable routes;
- limited space for incoming forces.

A broader Beachhead creates more maneuver options but also a larger perimeter.

The operational question is not simply:

> How many coastal hexes do I control?

It is:

> What can this lodgment support next?

## 13.4 Worked Example — Ashore but Unable to Break Out

Beachhead B remains intact.

Reinforcements can arrive.

But enemy formation X controls the main inland road.

Additional formations can accumulate near the coast without creating greater operational reach.

The next important action may therefore be to remove X and open the exit, not simply to land another formation.

## 13.5 Scenario-Data Boundary

Specific Beachhead values such as:

- capacity;
- persistence;
- loss conditions;
- supply capability;

remain Scenario-defined.

This Companion does not invent them.

The general concept to remember is:

**a Beachhead is not a “successful landing marker.” It is the mechanism that turns a landing into a sustainable land campaign.**

# 14. Victory Conditions

## 14.1 Read Victory First

Victory Conditions should be read before the first move.

They tell you:

- which places matter;
- what must be defended;
- which combats are worth taking;
- which losses may be acceptable.

## 14.2 Enemy Losses Are Usually a Means

Destroying enemy formations is powerful.

It is not automatically the objective.

A side can win many combats and still lose the campaign if it fails to control the required Objectives.

## 14.3 Timing Matters

Victory is checked at the defined time.

Holding an Objective briefly may not be enough.

This gives the opponent time to respond when the Scenario architecture requires it.

## 14.4 Worked Example — Capture Is Not Yet Victory

A side controls all required Objectives at the end of its own actions.

The Scenario checks victory only after the opponent's Player Turn.

The opponent recaptures one Objective.

The victory condition never became true at the official check.

The lesson is:

**capture and durable control are different capabilities.**

---

## 14.5 Plan Backward from the Victory Condition

After reading the Victory Condition, ask:

> What intermediate positions must exist before I can satisfy it reliably?

The answer may include locations that are not themselves Objectives:

- road junctions;
- supply routes;
- ports;
- reserve positions;
- approach corridors.

A Victory Condition therefore creates a chain of supporting operational problems.

## 14.6 Worked Example — Do Not Take the Shortest Route Automatically

Road A is the shortest route to Objective O.

It also leads directly into the enemy reserve position.

Road B is longer but passes through junction J.

If controlling J:

- restricts enemy reserve movement;
- improves your supply route;
- creates two approaches to O;

then the longer route may produce a more durable final position.

## 14.7 Worked Example — You Can Capture It but Cannot Yet Hold It

Formation A can reach Objective O now.

Friendly formations B and C are one turn behind.

Victory is checked only after the opponent's Player Turn.

If A moves alone, enemy reserve R may recapture O before the official check.

Waiting one turn for B and C may be stronger than immediate occupation.

Victory is not simply about reaching the Objective first.

It is about creating the map state that exists at the formal check.

## 14.8 Local Victory Can Still Lose the Campaign

Suppose your main force spends three turns defeating powerful enemy formation X.

The fight is successful.

During those turns, the opponent secures the Objectives that actually matter for victory.

Destroying X was meaningful tactically and operationally.

It may still have been the wrong use of time.

Every combat result should eventually be translated back into the language of the Victory Condition.

## 14.9 Common Victory Misreadings

### “If I take all required Objectives, I win immediately.”

Check the official timing.

### “Destroying more enemy units means I am winning.”

Only if that supports the actual Victory Condition.

### “Non-objective locations do not matter.”

Supporting roads, ports, Entry Sources, and reserve positions can determine whether Objectives can be held.

## 14.10 Advanced Reading — Victory Conditions Are the Evaluation Function

Two identical board positions can have different strategic value under different Victory Conditions.

Victory rules do not merely score the game at the end.

They tell the player how every other system should be valued.

That is why they should be read before play and revisited whenever a decision feels ambiguous.

# 15. Game End Conditions

The game does not automatically end because the board feels static.

General end paths include:

- Scenario-defined decisive victory;
- concession;
- mutual draw agreement.

A human or artificial intelligence controlling a player role may make those decisions for that role under the same player authority.

## 15.1 Why There Is No Generic Automatic Stalemate Rule

A quiet map can still contain meaningful preparation:

- supply restoration;
- reinforcement arrival;
- reserve movement;
- preparation for a breakthrough.

A universal “nothing happened for several turns, therefore stop” rule could end a game before the operational situation is actually resolved.

---

## 15.2 Decisive Victory and Concession Are Different Kinds of End

A decisive victory is a Scenario-defined game state.

Concession is a player decision.

The first says:

> The formal campaign condition has been reached.

The second says:

> I judge that continuing no longer offers a realistic path to success.

Keeping them distinct lets the game end through either formal board-state resolution or legitimate player judgment.

## 15.3 A Draw Is Not an Automatic “Nobody Can Win” State

A mutual draw requires agreement between the player roles.

A quiet board does not automatically become a draw.

The apparent stalemate may conceal:

- incoming reinforcements;
- supply recovery;
- reserve redeployment;
- preparation for a new offensive.

Static appearance is not itself a Game End Condition.

## 15.4 Worked Example — Three Quiet Turns

Neither side conducts a major attack for three turns.

One side is using the time to:

- collect reinforcements;
- secure roads;
- improve supply;
- reposition reserves.

The map may look quiet.

Operationally, the situation may be changing substantially.

A generic “nothing happened, end the game” rule would misread preparation as irrelevance.

## 15.5 Human and Artificial-Intelligence Players Have the Same Role Authority

The controller of a player role may be human or artificial intelligence.

That does not create different game authorities.

The same player role can:

- concede;
- propose a draw;
- accept or decline a draw.

The controller type does not change the role's legal authority or accountability.

## 15.6 Advanced Reading — Game End Defines What Counts as a Resolved Campaign

Game End Conditions are not merely administrative cleanup.

They express when the design considers the operational contest meaningfully resolved.

That is why they belong conceptually beside Victory Conditions rather than as an afterthought.

# 16. Combined Example — Opening a Road Does Not Rewrite Supply Immediately

Formation P is Unsupplied at the current Supply Checkpoint.

Friendly formation Q later moves and opens the blocked road.

The board now shows a possible route.

P remains in its current supply state for the period governed by that checkpoint.

If the road remains open until the next relevant Supply Checkpoint, P may then qualify for supply.

This one situation connects:

- timing;
- movement;
- control;
- supply;
- planning across turns.

---

# 17. Combined Example — Combat Order Creates a Breakthrough

Attacking formations A, B, and C face defenders X and Y.

A+B attack X first.

X retreats.

A advances after combat and changes the local Zone of Control pattern.

Before attacking Y, the player reassesses:

- Y's retreat route;
- the road behind Y;
- whether C still needs to attack at all.

Combat sequence is therefore part of operational play.

---

# 18. Combined Example — Air Support Now or Interdiction for Later

An important combat could use Tactical Air Points for support.

Enemy reserve R is also positioned to use Operational Movement.

Air support improves the current attack.

Operational Interdiction may instead delay R.

The player is choosing between:

- greater immediate combat certainty; and
- a more favorable future map.

---

# 19. Combined Example — Win the Battle, Lose the Operational Position

Attacker A forces defender X to retreat.

A advances deeply.

Friendly B has taken losses and remains behind.

The only supply road is exposed.

Enemy reserve R can approach A's flank.

The combat result was favorable.

The operational result may be dangerous.

This is why the system separates:

**winning a combat**
from
**making a good campaign decision.**

---

# 20. Full Integrated Example — Thinking Through an Entire Player Turn

This example uses generic formations and locations rather than Scenario-specific names or values.

## 20.1 Starting Position

Friendly formations A and B face enemy formation X.

Friendly reserve C stands on a road behind them.

Reinforcement R is expected to become Available next turn.

Behind X lies road junction Y.

Enemy reserve Z can approach Y from another sector.

A later Victory Objective lies beyond Y.

## 20.2 Supply Checkpoint

A, B, and C are Supplied.

A and B rely heavily on one road.

The player therefore begins the turn by thinking not only:

> Can I attack?

but also:

> Can I still trace a useful route next turn?

## 20.3 Movement Decision

Moving C forward would strengthen the immediate attack.

Keeping C near the road protects the logistics route and future Entry access for R.

The player keeps C back.

This gives up some immediate combat strength in exchange for operational stability.

## 20.4 Air Allocation

Enemy reserve Z may use Operational Movement to reinforce the sector.

Tactical Air Points could strengthen the attack against X.

Instead, the player uses air resources to interfere with Z.

The purpose is to buy time after the breakthrough.

## 20.5 Combat

A and B attack X.

X retreats.

A may advance.

A chooses not to use the maximum possible Post-Combat Advance because the deepest position would stretch supply and separate A from B.

Again, the player gives up distance to preserve future options.

## 20.6 Reassess the Map

X has moved.

The approach to Y is more open.

Z is less able to react immediately.

C still protects the road.

R is expected next turn.

The player can now imagine a new plan:

> A+B+R may be able to pressure Y next turn while C secures the rear.

## 20.7 Think Ahead to the Next Supply Checkpoint

A deeper advance this turn would make that future plan less stable.

The player stops.

The strongest operational decision is not to use every legal opportunity immediately.

## 20.8 Return to Victory

Y is not itself the final Objective.

But controlling Y may:

- open more routes;
- restrict enemy reserve movement;
- improve access to the later Objective.

The attack on X therefore mattered because it changed the campaign geometry.

## 20.9 What Systems Were Used?

The example involved:

- supply;
- movement;
- roads;
- reserves;
- Tactical Air;
- combat;
- retreat;
- Post-Combat Advance;
- reinforcement;
- victory planning.

The player did not need to think of those as ten unrelated chapters.

The player asked one operational question:

> **Which choices this turn leave me with the strongest position next turn?**

That is the central habit this Companion is trying to teach.

# 21. Reading Scenario-Specific and Supporting Systems

Common rules do not force every campaign to use the same historical sub-systems.

## 21.1 Rail Features

A rail line on the map does not automatically create generic rail movement or rail capacity.

If a Scenario needs rail behavior, it must define it.

## 21.2 Weather

The common rules do not require a universal random weather table every turn.

A Scenario may express weather through explicit events, resources, readiness, or other Scenario-defined mechanisms.

## 21.3 Naval Combat and Sea Redeployment

The existence of amphibious operations does not imply a general naval combat game or unrestricted sea redeployment.

Those capabilities require explicit rules if a Scenario needs them.

## 21.4 Do Not Create a Subsystem for Every Historical Detail

Historical importance does not automatically justify a new player-facing subsystem.

A special rule is most valuable when:

- the existing system cannot express the issue adequately; and
- the added rule creates a meaningful player decision.

# 22. Common Misreadings

## 22.1 “I still have Movement Points, so I can keep moving.”

Not necessarily.

Zone of Control or other conditions can end movement independently of remaining Movement Points.

## 22.2 “The road is open now, so I am immediately Supplied.”

Not necessarily.

Supply state is established at the relevant Supply Checkpoint.

## 22.3 “A reinforcement is Available, so it appears on the map immediately.”

No.

Entry Source and capacity still matter.

## 22.4 “Replacement resources can restore any eliminated formation.”

Not by default.

Reconstitution requires explicit authorization.

## 22.5 “Tactical Air Points and Heavy Breakthrough Bombardment are the same air resource.”

No.

They are distinct resources with different operational meaning.

## 22.6 “A successful landing creates a permanent Supply Source.”

No.

Beachhead state and Scenario data matter.

## 22.7 “The side that destroys more enemy formations wins.”

Not necessarily.

Read the Victory Conditions.

## 22.8 “If the Scenario has not supplied a value, use a reasonable default.”

No.

Unresolved data remain unresolved.

## 22.9 “The best move is the one that goes farthest.”

Not necessarily.

Supply, reserve response, retreat routes, and next-turn position may make a shorter move stronger.

## 22.10 “If I can attack, I should attack.”

Not necessarily.

Ask what the combat changes operationally.

# 23. Interpretation Checklist

When something feels unclear, use this order.

1. Check the Player Rulebook.
2. If the issue is campaign-specific, check the Scenario Book.
3. Use this Companion to understand why the rule behaves that way.
4. If a value is missing, determine whether it belongs to common rules or Scenario data.
5. Do not invent a value marked unresolved.
6. Confirm the current Step.
7. Separate current state from what the map would produce if recalculated now.
8. After combat, re-check retreat, advance, Zone of Control, roads, and supply.
9. Imagine the next relevant checkpoint.
10. Return to the Victory Conditions.

# 25. System Interaction Map

Once the individual systems make sense, begin looking for the links between them.

## 25.1 Movement → Zone of Control → Combat

Movement determines where contact exists.

Contact determines which attacks are possible and which routes become restricted.

Combat therefore begins before the die is rolled.

## 25.2 Combat → Retreat → Zone of Control → Next Combat

One retreat can alter the Zone of Control pattern.

That can change a later combat.

Sequential resolution exists so that the player can respond to that changing map.

## 25.3 Post-Combat Advance → Supply

A breakthrough extends the front.

It may also extend the supply route.

The question after a successful combat is not simply:

> How far can I advance?

It is:

> How far can I advance and still operate effectively next turn?

## 25.4 Roads → Supply → Reinforcement

A road can support:

- movement;
- supply;
- access from Entry Sources.

A road junction may therefore matter even when no combat occurs there this turn.

## 25.5 Air → Enemy Reserve → Future Combat

Operational Interdiction may not damage the unit you care about.

It may still prevent that unit from arriving in time to matter.

## 25.6 Airborne → Link-Up → Supply

Early position is valuable only if the airborne force can survive long enough for the wider operation to exploit it.

## 25.7 Amphibious Assault → Beachhead → Reinforcement

Getting ashore is the beginning.

The operational question is whether that landing becomes a sustainable entry and supply position.

## 25.8 Victory Conditions Sit at the End of Every Chain

Movement, combat, supply, air, and reinforcement are means.

Victory tells you which uses of those means matter.

# 26. Decision Training — How to Think Through Common Situations

## 26.1 The Enemy Is Weak. Should I Attack?

Ask:

- What opens if I win?
- What do I risk?
- Does this enemy actually block anything important?
- Is there a more valuable use of the same force?

Weak does not automatically mean important.

## 26.2 A Road Is Open. Should I Rush Forward?

Check:

- future supply;
- enemy Zone of Control;
- reserve response;
- flank protection;
- retreat routes.

An open route is an option, not an order.

## 26.3 I Have a Reserve. Should I Commit It?

A committed reserve strengthens one area.

It also gives up the ability to respond somewhere else.

Reserve commitment is the act of reducing future choice.

## 26.4 An Unsupplied Formation Needs Help. Should I Rescue It?

Compare:

- the value of the formation;
- the forces required to rescue it;
- the direction of the main effort;
- the position you will occupy after the rescue.

Saving a formation can still damage the campaign plan.

## 26.5 I Can Capture the Objective. Should I?

Only if you can evaluate the official victory-check timing.

A temporary occupation may not be enough.

## 26.6 I Can Advance After Combat. Should I?

Check supply, support, reserve exposure, and retreat routes.

Legal does not mean wise.

## 26.7 I Have Air Resources Left. Should I Spend Them?

Ask whether a later moment may be more decisive.

Scarce resources are often about timing rather than permission.

## 26.8 Reinforcements Are Arriving. Should They Go Straight to the Front?

Perhaps.

But they may be more valuable protecting:

- an Entry Source;
- a road;
- a logistics route;
- a reserve position.

# 28. Knowing a Rule versus Being Able to Use It

Memorizing:

> entering enemy Zone of Control ends Regular Movement

lets you process movement.

Understanding:

> Zone of Control can be used to close roads and shape enemy movement

lets you use the rule operationally.

Memorizing:

> supply is checked at a checkpoint

lets you process supply.

Understanding:

> opening a road this turn may prepare next turn's offensive

lets you plan.

Memorizing:

> combat uses a band and shifts

lets you resolve a battle.

Understanding:

> combat order can open routes and change later attacks

lets you command the operation.

The final question to ask after any proposed action is:

> **If this works, what becomes better for my victory plan?**

If you cannot explain the answer, the action may not be necessary.

The goal of this Companion is not to replace the Player Rulebook in your memory.

It is to help you use the Player Rulebook correctly without needing to memorize everything.

# 29. Advanced Cross-System Cases

These cases are not new rules.

They are exercises in deciding which existing rule relationship matters most.

## 29.1 The Best Combat Result Creates the Worst Supply Position

You have two possible attacks.

Attack A is more favorable and is likely to force the defender back.

Attack B is less favorable but would open the road that supplies the entire forward sector.

If you evaluate only Combat Band, A looks better.

If you evaluate the operation, B may be far more important.

The correct order of thought is:

1. What changes on the map?
2. Which route changes?
3. Which formations become easier or harder to supply?
4. What does that enable next turn?
5. Only then ask which combat result is more attractive.

This is a recurring operational principle:

**a weaker combat can be the stronger campaign move.**

## 29.2 The Reserve That Does Nothing May Be Doing Its Job

Reserve R stands behind two sectors.

The opponent does not know which sector will receive R.

As long as R remains uncommitted, both enemy plans must account for its possible response.

If you send R into the first available combat merely because it can help, you gain strength in one place but remove uncertainty everywhere else.

The reserve has value before it fights.

Its location creates an operational threat.

This is why “unused” and “wasted” are not the same state.

## 29.3 A Unit Can Be Locally Safe and Operationally Trapped

Formation A holds strong terrain.

The enemy is unlikely to defeat it in a direct attack.

That looks safe.

But if enemy movement threatens the roads behind A, the formation may lose:

- supply;
- reinforcement support;
- practical retreat routes.

A good defensive hex can become a bad operational position.

When judging defense, ask not only:

> Can the enemy beat me here?

but also:

> Can I still function here if the map around me changes?

## 29.4 Reinforcement Timing Can Change the Value of a Road

Road junction J is not important this turn.

No current formation needs it.

Next turn, however, several reinforcements are expected to enter through Entry Source E, and J is the best route toward the threatened sector.

The road therefore has **future value**.

Operational games reward players who see infrastructure before the immediate need appears.

The same logic applies to:

- ports before major supply arrives;
- bridges before a reserve redeployment;
- Objectives before the official victory check.

## 29.5 A Victory Objective Can Be a Trap

Objective O is within reach.

Taking it would satisfy part of the victory condition.

However, the attacking force would have to advance into a narrow position with poor supply and strong enemy reserve access.

If the official check occurs only after the enemy response, the apparent opportunity may be a trap.

The correct question is not:

> Can I occupy O?

It is:

> Can I still control O at the official check time?

Victory timing converts tactical capture into an operational holding problem.

# 30. The Companion as a Thinking Tool

The purpose of a Companion is not to make the rules sound more complicated.

It should make the player's thinking simpler.

When a position becomes confusing, reduce it to four questions.

## 30.1 Time

- What Step is active?
- Which states were already fixed?
- What will be checked next?

## 30.2 Position

- Where are the important roads, Objectives, Entry Sources, and enemy ZOCs?
- Which formation is actually blocking the operation?

## 30.3 Continuity

- Can the force remain supplied?
- Can reinforcement reach it?
- Does the reserve still have useful options?

## 30.4 Purpose

- Which victory condition is this serving?
- If this action succeeds, what becomes possible that was not possible before?

If you can answer those four groups, most rules questions become much easier to place.

The exact procedure may still require the Player Rulebook.

But the operational problem will already be clear.

That is the distinction between knowing a rules system and being able to use it.
